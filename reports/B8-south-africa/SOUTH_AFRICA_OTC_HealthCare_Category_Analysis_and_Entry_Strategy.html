C1 · Category frame — a big wellness market wearing three different size labels

South Africa's complementary-and-wellness economy is large and badly measured: Traditional & Complementary Medicine at US$1.40bn (2025) → $2.13bn by 2033 @5.6% (Grand View) versus a narrower CAM cut at $764M (2024) → $2.97bn @16.3% (IMARC) — a definitional spread this deck carries unblended [!] Euromonitor/Nielsen ZAR re-derive — plus dietary supplements at US$1.06bn (2024) @9.9%. Call the arena ZAR 19–38bn depending on definition, growing high-single to mid-double digits. Three structural facts organise the entry. First, the regulatory gift: SAHPRA formally names AYURVEDA among six recognised CAM disciplines (Category D, GN 859/2017), with a low-risk traditional-use lane (D1.2) and a lighter Health Supplements lane permitting structure/function claims — the only market in this programme, and one of very few anywhere, where Dabur's formulation tradition is written into law. Second, the proven path: Himalaya holds 46 listings at Dis-Chem — face care, Ashwagandha, Septilin, Koflet, baby — an Indian Ayurvedic multinational already walked this exact road. Third, the demand weather: the domestic OTC king (Adcock Ingram — Panado, Corenza C, Bioplus, ~20% IQVIA share in its categories) reports demand softness "driven by stressed lower-income consumers" (Financial Mail, May 2025), while the state's Health Promotion Levy has taxed sugar since 2018 with measured effect — the sugar-free-first doctrine's first market with government policy on its side.

C2 · Demand architecture — four wellness wallets

Wallet one: the pharmacy-trust wellness buyer. Half of CAM turnover moves through pharmacies; Clicks (780 pharmacies) and Dis-Chem are where South Africans convert health anxiety into baskets, guided by pharmacists and shelf adjacency. Himalaya's 46 listings prove this wallet opens for Ayurvedic products with clean claims. Wallet two: the immune-season household. Winter (June–August) drives cold-flu and immunity spending; the shelf already holds an INDIGENOUS botanical immune set — African Potato, Sutherlandia, Buchu — meaning "traditional-origin remedy in modern pharmacy" is a NORMALISED category Chyawanprash joins rather than creates. Wallet three: the stressed value-seeker. Adcock's own commentary names the squeeze; a multi-benefit daily tonic priced honestly against a basket of single-purpose supplements is a consolidation offer to a consumer doing painful arithmetic. Wallet four: the metabolic manager. Whatever the true prevalence figure (4.5–15.25%), diabetes is a mass reality with a state tax dramatising sugar daily at every till — the sugar-free tonic's audience does its own marketing. And beside all four, the RESPECTFUL-DISTANCE zone: the muthi/traditional-healing economy (sangoma/inyanga, legally recognised) is a parallel system Dabur must never imitate, reference, or crowd — Ayurveda positions as a FELLOW traditional system from elsewhere, beside the indigenous shelf, never above it panel-gated positioning review.

The consumer decode in one story

A July morning in two queues. At a Dis-Chem in Centurion, a 41-year-old logistics manager waits for his chronic-medication collection — metformin, since last year's diagnosis — and passes the wellness bays twice. He has money for prevention and no map for it: the vitamin wall is alphabet soup, Biogen looks like it's for gym people, the African Potato bottle is his mother's remedy not his, and nothing on any shelf says "for a man whose numbers just changed". He buys a multivitamin he won't finish. In a Phoenix (Durban) kitchen, a grandmother stirs a spoon of Chyawanprash into warm milk for her grandson before school — the jar came from the corner grocer, priced whatever the importer felt that month — while her daughter, a nurse, quietly wishes the jar had a label she could trust at work: a proper SA registration, an ingredients panel her colleagues would respect, a sugar-free version for her father with the same diagnosis as the man in Centurion.

Read together: the market's biggest unserved wellness buyer is a newly-diagnosed metabolic manager with no daily-prevention format offered to him — and the product that would serve him already exists in the country, informally imported, trusted by the one household cohort that knows it, and wished into respectability by a nurse who represents exactly the pharmacist-trust channel this deck routes through. The dossier, the desk and the sugar-free variant connect the two queues.

3.1 Adcock Ingram — the domestic king, complaining audibly

Adcock's OTC portfolio (Panado R36.99–58.99, Corenza C list 175.99/special 84.99, Compral, Med-Lemon R64.79, Bioplus tonics, ViralGuard immunity, ProbiFlora) holds ~20% IQVIA share in pain/cold-flu/digestive — and its FY25 commentary names "stressed lower-income consumers" as a demand drag. Its entry teardown (decades of pharmacist trust + symptomatic clarity + local manufacture) built the fortress; its current posture is defensive pricing and portfolio maintenance. The exposed flank is PREVENTION: Adcock owns symptoms (pain, cold, flu) and rents immunity (ViralGuard is a line, not a philosophy); a daily-wellness tonic tradition attacks the calendar upstream of every Panado purchase. Lesson: do not fight the symptom king on symptom shelves — sell the season before the symptom.

3.2 Himalaya — the precedent, the neighbour, the ceiling-check

Forty-six Dis-Chem listings: face care (34), vitamins (28 incl. Ashwagandha 60s), baby (33), plus Septilin syrup and Koflet lozenges — a full Ayurvedic architecture trading TODAY at SA's #2 pharmacy chain, with claims language ("Even Tone", structure/function vitamins) that has evidently survived SAHPRA scrutiny. What it proves: the lane is real, the desk buys it, the consumer pays for it. What it warns: Himalaya got there first and owns the "Ayurvedic wellness" adjacency in the buyer's mental map — Dabur's pitch must be COMPLEMENTARY (Chyawanprash is a FORMAT Himalaya doesn't sell: the multi-herb daily jar) rather than substitutive, or the buyer hears "another Himalaya" and files the meeting under done. 3.3 Biogen — the landlord's own wellness empire

Dis-Chem built Biogen from house brand to national sports-nutrition leader with international distribution — the most successful own-brand play in SA health retail. Consequence one: the best bays at Dis-Chem have a house tenant. Consequence two, more useful: Dis-Chem has proven it INVESTS in wellness categories that turn — a supplier whose products move gets shelf, media and range support from a retailer that thinks like a brand-builder. The pitch leans into this: bring the desk a category story (Ayurvedic daily wellness) too authentic for a house brand to fake, with turn data from the wellness niche to prove it.

3.4 The indigenous botanical shelf — the neighbours to honour

African Potato (Hypoxis), Sutherlandia, Buchu: indigenous immune-and-vitality botanicals normalised in mainstream pharmacy [!] current brands/prices W0. They are not competitors to beat; they are the shelf's PROOF that South Africans buy traditional-origin wellness in modern formats — and the etiquette standard: these brands trade on indigenous heritage without costume or claim-inflation. Chyawanprash joins as the visiting tradition: same shelf, own story, no borrowed feathers. The muthi economy proper (sangoma/inyanga practice, the Durban muthi market) is OUT OF BOUNDS for marketing reference entirely — the respectful-distance rule is absolute and panel-enforced.

3.5 The honey shelf — a market that learned to distrust sweetness

South Africa consumes ~5,000t of honey against ~2,000t local production; ~76% of imports arrive from China; and the category's defining consumer narrative is ADULTERATION — the 2018 Carte Blanche "honey laundering" exposé through Daily Maverick's 2024–25 "Sweet deception" reporting. Genuine SA honey holds R65–90/500g; cheap blends undercut and erode trust further. The teardown lesson inverts the usual: in this category, the incumbent to displace is DOUBT itself. Any Dabur honey entry must lead with verifiable purity architecture — batch lab certificates, traceability, third-party testing published openly — or must not happen. That is the eighth honey derivation, formalised in C11.

C11 · The two flag-moves, argued

DEEP THINK — the regulatory door: gift, or trap for the impatient? SAHPRA naming Ayurveda looks like a red carpet; the 2022 court set-aside and the still-transitional roadmap say the carpet is being relaid while guests arrive. The impatient read: launch everything as "health supplements" (the lighter lane) and let Category D mature without us. The reckless read: file D1.2 dossiers immediately for the whole range and wait out the queue. The deck's read: SPLIT BY CLAIM AMBITION. SKUs whose story survives on structure/function language (classic Chyawanprash as daily wellness, honey as food) launch in the supplement/food lanes NOW at retail speed; the sugar-free R3, whose metabolic-dignity story benefits from stronger positioning over time, files D1.2 in parallel so that when Category D settles, Dabur holds registrations in the discipline the law names — a moat latecomers must queue for. Second-order: Himalaya presumably holds analogous positions; matching their regulatory footprint is table stakes, exceeding it in the D1.2 queue is the land grab. Kill-test: if the consultant's W0 review finds the transitional regime freezing new D-category intake, the split collapses to supplements-only — the deck still ships, the moat waits.
DEEP STRAT — the eighth honey derivation: PURITY-PROOF-OR-PASS. Seven verdicts exist: Kuwait's ladder, Uganda's no-go, Kenya's study, Tanzania's source-co-brand, Rwanda's pass-footnote, Saudi's format-entry, Qatar's respect-and-routine. South Africa's facts force an eighth: a 3,000-tonne import gap filled largely from China, a decade of laundering scandals, price inversion where CHEAP signals FAKE, and no dominant trusted brand — a category where the incumbent is doubt. Verdict: PURITY-PROOF-OR-PASS — enter only behind a verification architecture (batch-level lab certificates, QR-traceable origin, third-party testing published openly, price INSIDE the genuine band at R80–110) or do not enter at all. Works if: certification logistics land at acceptable unit cost and one duopoly desk buys the transparency story as differentiation. Fails if: certificates read as protesting too much, or the grey-import economics undercut faster than trust builds. Kill-switch: pass entirely, with the honey budget converting to the R3 sugar-free launch — and the pass documented as the eighth derivation's honest outcome, exactly as Rwanda's footnote was. The derivation's rule for the batch: in scandal-primed honey markets, the ONLY entry currency is verifiable proof, and a brand unwilling to fund proof should not spend the trust it brought from other categories.

What this deck teaches the batch

Two exports. First, the eighth honey derivation — PURITY-PROOF-OR-PASS — completes a taxonomy the batch's remaining files will draw from: Zambia, Mozambique and Angola each have honey economies (Zambia's is export-grade and forest-based) and each will get its own derivation, but the SCANDAL-MARKET rule derived here (where doubt is the incumbent, proof is the only currency) now exists as precedent. Second, the regulatory-split pattern — launch on the lighter lane, file the discipline lane in parallel — travels to every market whose CAM frameworks are mid-reform, which in this batch is most of them. South Africa's SAHPRA is the region's reference regulator; dossiers built to its standard downgrade gracefully everywhere else, never the reverse.

Reading the six moves as one sentence

Compressed: file where the law already knows your name, prove yourself on the shelf where the fussiest buyers shop, then walk into the retailer that rewards wellness brands that turn — with a jar format the proven precedent never brought, a sugar-free lead the state's own tax argues for, and a honey decision made by certificates or not at all. The refusals: no muthi-adjacent creative under any brief, ever (respectful distance is absolute); no immune-cure claims (post-COVID scar tissue + SAHPRA watchfulness); no honey without proof (the eighth derivation is binding); no launch into the wrong season (a winter product launched in November waits six months to matter — patience beats presence); and no undercutting the genuine-honey price band even with proof in hand, because the band IS the market's trust signal and breaking it re-teaches the doubt this deck exists to answer.
