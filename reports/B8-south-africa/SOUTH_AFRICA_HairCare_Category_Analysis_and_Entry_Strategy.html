C1 · Category frame — a big market mid-inflection, and Dabur is already inside

South Africa's retail hair-care market sits in a defensible public range of US$550–800M (ZAR ~10–15bn) depending on scope, growing ~2.4–5.9% by source [!] IMARC/Statista/GMR scope reconcile — Africa's most formalised hair market layered over a vast informal salon/braiding economy nobody has credibly sized source gap — field ask. The structural story is the natural-hair inflection: relaxer/perm retail sales fell ~20% (News24, 2018) as the natural-hair movement took urban, younger Black women out of chemical straightening and into texture care — oils, curl creams, leave-ins, growth serums; continental styling-product sales nearly doubled 2013–2015 while relaxers fell 18.6% vintage data [!] re-verify 2024/25 — a named source conflict exists on whether relaxers still grow in some markets. Dabur's position in that inflection is the file's headline: Vatika Afro Naturals — Coconut Restorative, Rosemary & Mint, Cactus, So Strong — purpose-built for Afro-textured hair, listed at Clicks, Dis-Chem and Takealot, every 100ml SKU deliberately under R100 (So Strong R99.99; Coconut R95.99 [!] cache), covered by Glamour SA. Beneath it, Dabur Amla trades at Dis-Chem (~R42.99/100ml) and Takealot (R77–95 — a dispersion that is itself a finding [!]), while Clicks runs an entire "Amla Oil" category page — the ingredient is genericising into the market's vocabulary faster than the brand is claiming it.

C2 · Demand architecture — three rituals, two economies

Ritual one: texture care (the growth engine). The natural-hair generation — urban, under 35, TikTok-taught, promo-literate — builds multi-step routines around moisture, curl definition, growth and scalp health. Oils are foundational to the ritual (pre-poo, sealing, scalp massage), which makes an oils-first company structurally lucky: Vatika did not have to convert anyone to oiling — the movement did it free. The buyer compares ingredients on TikTok before she compares brands on shelf; amla, rosemary, castor and coconut are already vocabulary. Ritual two: protective styling (the adjacent giant). Braids, weaves and wigs — Darling's empire — create their own care demands: scalp oils under braids, braid sprays, wash-day treatments between installs. Dabur does not sell extensions and should not; it should sell the CARE LAYER the extensions economy generates [!] field-size the braid-care basket. Ritual three: the household oil (the value floor). In lower-income households and the spaza economy, hair oil is a multi-purpose staple bought in small quantities at absolute-rand price points — the LUP lane the macro's two-economy doctrine mandates. Three rituals, one supply spine, two economies — and a fourth, smaller current: the KZN Indian household's amla tradition, which gives the brand its origin credibility but must never become its ceiling.

The consumer decode in one story

Wash day in Tembisa. A 27-year-old admin clerk — two years post-big-chop — starts Sunday morning with a pre-poo she learned from a Durban creator's TikTok: oil worked through dry hair before shampoo. The oil is a generic amla she picked up at Clicks because the creator said "amla" and the house brand was R15 cheaper than the one with the tree on it; she does not know the brand with the tree invented the category she is practising. Her mother, in the kitchen, keeps a tub of relaxer she has used since 1998 and a bottle of Isoplus; they argue gently about hair the way families argue about church. Her cousin arrives at noon to have knotless braids redone — six weeks of growth under the old set, scalp itchy, and the "braid spray" in her bag is a pharmacy antiseptic diluted in a spritz bottle because nothing on any shelf is actually made for the six weeks between installs.

Three women, one Sunday: the first is buying Dabur's ingredient without Dabur's brand — the claim-back programme in one purchase; the second is the declining ritual the deck refuses to chase; the third is improvising a product that does not exist — the braid-care column, demonstrated with a spritz bottle. The deck's six moves answer this kitchen.

C3 · Brand-by-brand teardown

3.0 Self-teardown: a working machine, an unmanaged ladder, an unaudited inheritance

Three own-assets, three different conditions. The working machine: Vatika Afro Naturals is the programme's single best pre-existing position in any market — a localised range (not a transplanted Gulf or India formula set), correct channel (the duopoly), correct price discipline (everything under R100), correct positioning language (inclusive, texture-first), with press coverage to prove intentionality. Whoever built this knew the market. The unmanaged ladder: Dabur Amla trades at ~R42.99 at Dis-Chem and R77–95 on Takealot — a 2× dispersion on the same 100ml that tells you nobody owns national price architecture; meanwhile Clicks' amla CATEGORY page hosts third-party amla oils, monetising Dabur's ingredient equity without Dabur's participation. The unaudited inheritance: the CTL acquisition's brand roster (Long Aid per the brief) cannot be verified from public sources [!] W0 internal/CIPC — there may be a third asset in the cupboard. The teardown's sentence: this is the first market in the programme where Dabur's problem is not absence or neglect but UNEVENNESS — one asset run well, one run loosely, one possibly forgotten.

3.1 L'Oréal / Dark & Lovely — the incumbent crown, aging

Dark & Lovely is the reference ethnic-hair megabrand — relaxer-era equity now stretched across an "Au Naturale" texture range. Its entry playbook (decades of salon education + mass distribution + aspirational advertising) built the category; its current exposure is structural: its core equity is welded to the declining relaxer ritual, and its texture-care extension fights credibility questions a born-natural brand never faces. Its defence is shelf power and brand memory. Lesson: do not fight D&L for the relaxer generation; win the generation that never relaxed — where incumbency is a liability, not an asset. [!] D&L ZAR ladder — store walk

3.2 Godrej (Darling, Inecto, Renew, Mega Growth) — the Indian empire next door

Godrej built Africa's dominant extensions business by acquiring local leaders (Kinky 2008, Darling 2010–11, Frika 2015), manufacturing at scale (Beluluane, ~5M hairpieces/month) and running brands AS African brands — SA alone is 35% of its Africa personal-care sales. It also owns Inecto (ethnic hair colour leader, 60+ years). What it proves: an Indian parent can lead SA ethnic hair when it commits manufacturing, local identity and category focus. What it leaves open: Godrej owns the HAIR you install; nobody owns the SCALP under it. The braid-care layer — oils, soothing sprays, wash-day treatments purpose-marketed for protective styles — is the flank a botanical-oils company is built to take.

3.3 Marico (Black Chic, Caivil, Isoplus) — the roll-up operator

Marico's SA model is buy-and-run-local: Black Chic (40 years of styling heritage), Caivil ("affordable everyday luxury"), Isoplus (2017, ~Rs36cr — "the Hair Doctor in a Can"). Its craft is portfolio discipline at value price points with local identity intact. It is the closest strategic mirror to what Dabur SHOULD be doing with the CTL inheritance — which makes it both the model and the most likely bidder for anything Dabur neglects. Lesson: the roll-up lane in SA hair is proven, active, and contested by a direct Indian peer. [!] Marico SA ZAR ladders — store walk

3.4 Black Like Me — the heritage lesson

Founded 1985 by Herman Mashaba on a R30,000 loan at apartheid's height; first Black-owned SA haircare company; own plant by 1990; still trading at its 40th anniversary under Connie Mashaba. It is not a scale threat to Dabur; it is a REGISTER lesson: in this market, ownership stories, struggle heritage and Black-consumer-first authenticity are not marketing garnish — they are the moral floor of the category. A foreign botanical brand earns its place through respect, formulation truth and local jobs (the plant matters), never through borrowed struggle language. The macro's register doctrine (T5b) applies to hair advertising with full force.

3.5 ORS / TCB / Sta-Sof-Fro / Sofn'free — the specialist middle

The US-heritage and local specialist brands (ORS Olive Oil, TCB Naturals with a dedicated SA storefront, Pretoria's Sta-Sof-Fro "No.1 for natural-hair styling", GroHealthy's Sofn'free) populate the R40–90 texture-care middle where Vatika Afro Naturals trades. None has Vatika's ingredient-heritage depth (real botanical formulation lineage); most out-gun it on range breadth (creams, custards, sprays — Vatika SA is oils-led). The contest in this middle is therefore FORMAT EXTENSION: whoever completes the wash-day system (oil + cream + leave-in + treatment) at one trusted price band consolidates the routine. 3.6 Moroccanoil and the premium imports — the authenticity ceiling

Moroccanoil trades at Clicks and Dis-Chem with specialist stockists loudly certifying "100% authentic — authorised stockist": premium hair oil has a counterfeit problem serious enough that authenticity claims are a NORM, not a differentiator. Two consequences: the R200+ serum tier exists (a later-wave option, not this deck's move), and any Dabur scale-up must build authenticity infrastructure — consistent national pricing, serialised supply, retailer-verified channels — because the counterfeit economy (hair categories named in SAPS seizures) attacks whatever grows.

C11 · The two flag-moves, argued

DEEP THINK — the amla claim-back: own the ingredient or lose the brand. Clicks hosting an "Amla Oil" category page with third-party brands is the market saying: amla graduated from brand to ingredient. The naive responses are both wrong — litigating the word (unwinnable, alienating) or ignoring it (private label eats the value rung within two ranging cycles). The correct response is the CeraVe lesson inverted: become the ingredient's teacher. Dabur is the company that industrialised amla for hair a century ago; that story, told as education (what the fruit is, why the oil works, how to use it in a texture routine), makes every generic amla listing an advertisement for the brand that owns the story's origin. Second-order: private label continues to exist — and functions as Dabur's discount tier, paid for by retailers. Kill-test: if 12 months of claim-back content moves no brand-preference needle against private-label amla at the duopoly, the value rung repositions on FORMAT (the sachet/LUP lane private label won't chase) and cedes the commodity 100ml.
DEEP STRAT — the braid-care column: building beside the giant. Sequence: (1) W0 field study — the under-braid care basket, sized in salons and townships (what wearers actually buy, improvise, or skip between installs) field ask; (2) three-SKU line — under-braid scalp oil (light, applicator-tip), soothing braid spray, wash-day deep treatment — priced R70–130, formulated from the existing botanical bench; (3) route: duopoly shelf PLUS salon wholesale seeding via braid-educator partnerships (the ritual's authority figures); (4) content: the educator programme carries "scalp health under protective styles" as a service narrative, not an ad; (5) measure: attach rate to braid installs in pilot salons, then scale. Works if: the field study confirms the basket is real and unconsolidated. Fails if: Godrej bundles care SKUs with Darling installs first — the one competitor structurally positioned to close the column overnight. Kill-switch: if Darling moves first, pivot from column-builder to best-ingredient-supplier in the column (co-existence at R4 price points), and take the learning to Mozambique/Namibia where the same column is emptier.

C16 · What does not transplant

Four stay-homes, two arrivals. The GCC premium doctrine stays home entirely — the sachet is a channel's native format here, and refusing it would refuse 26% of the market. The Gulf two-register architecture does not map — South Africa's registers are not expatriate-vs-national but ritual-generational (natural vs relaxer eras) and economic (formal vs informal), and the deck's register table is built on those axes instead. India's amla mass-media playbook (celebrity TVCs) stays home; the authority here is the creator-educator, not the film star. Qatar's serum up-ladder stays home this wave — the counterfeit tax on premium imports makes the tier structurally hostile until authenticity rails exist. What arrives: the promo-floor law, reinterpreted for a specials-culture market (govern the real price, honest lists); and the self-teardown standard, which here found not neglect but unevenness — the most fixable diagnosis in the programme.
