THE BANNER. Brazilian oral care is the most concentrated category the programme has met in forty-four markets: Colgate holds ~70% share and sits in 95% of Brazilian households — the compounding legacy of its 1997 Kolynos acquisition (US$1.04bn, won against P&G; Kolynos alone held ~52% pre-merger, per the CADE competition-authority case history). The corridor diagnostic returns its twenty-sixth finding: FORTRESS-NICHED — a category whose centre is unassailable but whose walls leak at the niches the fortress ignores: Boni Natural proves a fluoride-free, vegan, 97.8% plant-derived toothpaste can hold D2C + Amazon.com.br + Mercado Livre + Ultrafarma distribution without troubling the giant. The niche's live vocabulary is sem flúor + vegano + natural-local — NOT ayurvedic (no ayurvedic toothpaste has meaningful Brazilian distribution; Meswak's footprint is zero). FORTRESS-NICHED corridors prescribe the directive's first PASSENGER ENTRY: oral rides the hair deck's infrastructure — the same AFE-holder, the same official Mercado Livre store, the same creator relationships — at near-zero marginal cost, listing Dabur Red and Meswak as the natural niche's first ayurvedic occupants, with copy in the niche's own vocabulary. And the deck documents the directive's first HONEST PASS: no physical-retail programme, no oral-specific marketing budget, no share ambition — the arithmetic (a 70% fortress + a >50% tax stack + a null estate) does not support a founding, and pretending otherwise would tax the hair deck's budget to decorate a spreadsheet. The passenger rides; the fortress is left alone; the pass is written down so nobody has to re-derive it annually.

The fortress deserves one paragraph of history because the history IS the strategy lesson. In the 1980s and early 1990s Brazil's toothpaste market had a genuine national champion: Kolynos held roughly 52% — a share built over decades of Brazilian retail presence — against Colgate's ~27%. The 1997 acquisition (US$1.04 billion for Kolynos's operations across 14 countries, won in a contested bid against P&G) did not create a market leader; it MERGED the top two, and the CADE competition file records the arithmetic: a combined share above 70% that has essentially held for nearly thirty years. The lesson for any entrant is that this dominance was never won on the shelf and therefore cannot be contested on the shelf — it was won in a boardroom transaction that predates most of today's shoppers' brand memories, and the only strategies that have coexisted with it since are the ones (Sensodyne's clinical specialism, Boni's conviction niche) that never asked the default-setting shopper to reconsider anything.

The waterfall's structural point generalises into the deck's second instrument, stated as a test any future category plan can run. An entry qualifies as a PASSENGER only if three conditions hold together: its incremental fixed costs round to zero on infrastructure another category has already funded and justified; its contribution margin is positive at any volume above zero (nothing to amortise means nothing to chase); and its failure mode is a quiet delisting rather than a write-off. Miss any one and the entry is a FOUNDING wearing a passenger's costume — the most dangerous object in portfolio planning, because it books a founding's risks against a passenger's scrutiny. Brazil oral passes all three tests only because Brazil hair is paying for the vehicle; the same SKUs in a market without a funded founding would need their own honest-pass memo.

The calendar's annual row is the deck's most unusual feature and deserves its defence: no other category plan in the directive schedules its own re-litigation. The reason is the pass itself — a documented refusal decays differently from a documented plan. Plans are killed by failure, visibly; refusals are killed by drift, silently, as personnel change and the arithmetic that justified them fades from institutional memory. The annual re-read, with minutes, is the cheapest known defence: fifteen minutes a year re-affirming (or, on evidence, re-opening) a decision that would otherwise be re-fought from scratch by people who never read the original file. DEEP THINK · Three objections answered

Objection 1 — "A passenger entry with no ambition is strategy theatre: why write 6,000 words to list two SKUs?" Because the words buy two things the listing alone cannot. First, the REFUSALS: without the documented pass, this category becomes the annual temptation — every optimistic review cycle will rediscover "the world's fifth-biggest oral market" and propose funding it; the memo with its arithmetic is the antibody, and it needs the full decode (the fortress's history, the stack, the null) to survive scrutiny. Second, the TEST DESIGN: the passenger listing is the cheapest demand experiment the programme has ever run on a genuinely open question (does ANY Brazilian want ayurvedic toothpaste?), and cheap experiments are only valuable when their success criteria are written before the data arrives. The deck is short on ambition and long on discipline because that is what this category's truth demands.

Objection 2 — "If the niche is real and growing, the honest pass may cost the real prize — Boni's shelf tomorrow is bigger than Boni's shelf today." The memo binds until evidence, not despite it — that is its precise design. The annual re-read exists because the niche COULD grow (a content-driven natural-toothpaste wave is entirely plausible in the TikTok Shop era), and the passenger seat means Dabur is already aboard if it does: listings live, reviews accruing, the pharmacy file pre-created and data-gated. The pass refuses FUNDING, not presence. What it protects against is the opposite error — funding shelf ambitions in a category where the demand question is still unanswered, with money the hair founding needs.

Objection 3 — "Selling fluoride-free toothpaste at 2.5× the mass price to a country where fluoride is public-health orthodoxy — is the passenger seat even responsible?" The deck holds the same line every market has held: the label states the formula's nature (sem flúor is a factual descriptor, mandatory anyway), the copy never argues against fluoride or public-health guidance (the no-argument law's 7th application), and the niche's existing shoppers are deliberate label-readers making an informed choice a legal product serves. Dabur adds no anti-fluoride voice to Brazil — it adds a herb-based option to a shelf that already exists, priced for deliberators, silent on everyone else's tube.
