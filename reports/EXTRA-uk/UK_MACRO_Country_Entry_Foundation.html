M1 · Engagement frame — an old estate, a new company, and a dated door

The UK is Dabur's oldest overseas footprint in this programme — and the census found it mid-reorganisation. Three UK registrations tell the story in filings: Dabur India Ltd's UK establishment (FC015949, opened 15 May 1991 — a 34-year presence), Dabur International Ltd's Isle-of-Man-domiciled UK establishment (FC026369, since 1 July 2005 — the offshore distribution era), and — the finding that frames everything — Dabur UK Trading Limited (company 16394633), incorporated 17 April 2025 at Park Royal, West London (the distribution estate that serves Southall), as a step-down subsidiary of Dabur International FZE, confirmed in Dabur's own FY2024-25 Annual Report. Fourteen months later — eight days from this report's date — the UK–India FTA enters into force (15 July 2026), eliminating tariffs on 99% of Indian exports to the UK. The deck states the obvious hypothesis exactly as carefully as the evidence allows: the new trading entity reads as deliberate on-shoring of import/compliance infrastructure ahead of the tariff step-down — plausible, unconfirmed by any source, and strategically correct whether or not it was the motive. The estate is not drifting; someone is already building what this report would have recommended. The macro's job shifts accordingly: not "what should exist" but what the new structure should do first, and in what order.

M2 · Market & demography — the largest diaspora market in the programme's West

The 2021 Census (England & Wales) counts 5.5M Asian/Asian British residents (9.3%) — up from 4.2M/7.5% in 2011, the largest rise of any broad group — within which Indian origin is 1.9M (3.1%), Pakistani ~1.6M, Bangladeshi 644,900 (1.1%): a South Asian population of ~4.1–4.2M in England & Wales alone, before Scotland and NI (full UK total is a T-queue row). Unlike the US's corridor geography, the UK's diaspora retail is a decades-old INSTITUTION: Leicester's Belgrave Road Golden Mile (evidenced directly — trading since the 1960s, the largest Diwali outside India), Southall, Birmingham's Soho Road, Green Street, Bradford, Rusholme (directionally standing, each queued for corridor-level re-verification). And the mainstream shell is closer here than anywhere the programme has been: the "world foods" aisle is standing architecture at Tesco and Sainsbury's — yet the census's most important single finding is that Dabur's hair oils don't sit there: they sit in the health-and-beauty aisle, listed as ordinary hair-care SKUs. In 37 prior markets the programme asked how a Dabur product earns a mainstream shelf; in the UK it already has one.

M6 · Demand engine — the oiling trend's home away from home

The UK's hair-oiling moment is bigger and more culturally loaded than the US version, and the deck reads both halves. The commercial half: TikTok Shop UK is the 4th-largest beauty retailer in the country; the oiling ritual has already produced a South-Asian-founder UK brand at documented scale (ByErim — 700k+ followers, ~$4M sales, CNBC Apr 2025); the hashtag economy is enormous. The cultural half, which the programme handles with care: UK/US media document a live appropriation tension — South Asian women who were mocked for oiled hair as children watching the same ritual celebrated when performed by others under "clean girl" vocabulary. For Dabur — the ritual's most literal originator brand — this discourse is both an asset and a duty of care: the brand can stand with the community's own telling (the origin credited, the diaspora's creators centred) and must never posture as a discovery brand. Diaspora media completes the engine: Sunrise Radio (425k weekly, Dec 2023 RAJAR), Zee TV UK (beating Colors on monthly reach, Jul 2025) — alive and measured, unlike most markets' diaspora-media guesswork.

M7 · The wellness substrate — a real registration route and an open review

Three substrate facts shape the OTC deck. First, the THR scheme means the UK is the one market where "traditional herbal medicine" is a REGULATORY CATEGORY, not just a marketing register — with the honest caveat that the framework suits few-herb European traditions (A.Vogel-class) better than 40-herb classicals. Second, the ashwagandha wave is here (~US$70M, double-digit growth) AND under an open FSA safety assessment (Sept 2024, unresolved) — the demand and the caveat arrive together, and the OTC deck plans both outcomes rather than betting on one. Third, the UK's Ayurveda-adjacent mainstream proof is Pukka — Bristol-born, herbal-first, mults- and H&B-distributed, carried from Unilever (2017) to CVC's Lipton Teas & Infusions (2021): proof that herbal-tradition branding clears UK mainstream gates at scale, and a corrected fact (the brief's Unilever assumption retired).
