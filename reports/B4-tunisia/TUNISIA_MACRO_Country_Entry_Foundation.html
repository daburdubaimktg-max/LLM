M1 — Demography

So what: B4's smallest market (12.4M) and its oldest (median 34.1) — a coastal, educated, French-inflected consumer with the Maghreb's deepest parapharmacie habit, living through a debt-and-FX squeeze that makes value-for-money the era's ruling emotion. The prize is not scale; it is a high-quality beachhead whose para channel and French-forward register rehearse nothing else in the batch.

Population 12.415M (IMF 2026); urbanisation ~70–72%; median age 34.1 (up from 29.9 in 2012 — the Maghreb's fastest-aging consumer). Geography is two coasts and a lagging interior: Greater Tunis ≈3M; the Sahel corridor (Sousse–Monastir–Mahdia–Sfax) ≈2.85M — together nearly half the country on ~15% of its land, with interior governorates falling behind on jobs and income (World Bank regional- disparities work). Internet penetration 84.9% (10.5M); social 7.25M with TikTok now the largest platform (5.18M adults), Instagram 3.45M, Facebook the commerce/groups layer (DataReportal early 2025). Unemployment 15.7% (youth ~40% in risk commentary [!] INS figure); SMIG 528 TND/month (48h, 2026). Language reality: Tounsi/Derja spoken, but French is the commercial register's senior partner — premium and pharmacy messaging runs French-first, a genuine flip from every Arabic-first market in B2/B3/B9 and stronger than Morocco/Algeria.

M2 — Economy (the squeeze)

So what: Tunisia's macro is the batch's most fragile OUTSIDE a war zone: debt 82% of GDP, no IMF programme by presidential choice, a €750M Eurobond due THIS MONTH (July 2026), reserves near ~106 days of imports — and the state's chosen shock absorber is exactly the instrument that hits this entry: import compression of "non-priority" goods, cosmetics named. The TND is managed-stable (~2.95/USD, −3.5% y/y) with no confirmed Algeria-style parallel market [!] — the risk here is not a two-price economy but a financing chokehold.

The debt clock's mechanics, spelled out: Tunisia chose sovereignty over programme money — Saied rejected IMF conditionality (subsidy cuts, wage restraint) that the UGTT would have fought in the street, which leaves the state servicing external debt from reserves and squeezing imports to protect them. The €750M Eurobond due this July is the near-term stress test: paid from reserves, it thins the buffer further and raises the odds the non-priority list tightens; restructured, it triggers a different cascade (rating events, trade-credit pricing). Either path keeps import compression ON as the state's instrument of choice for quarters — which is why this deck treats the BCT circular not as a passing rule but as the operating climate, and designs the entry to live in it rather than wait it out. The consumption base itself is resilient in the way stressed middle-income markets are: essentials defended, trade-down active, value brands gaining — the exact weather a value-herbal entrant prefers (strategist read).

The planning consequence is specific: the entry's binding cost is working capital, not duty. With the Agadir lane at 0% duty (M5), the real tax is the BCT's 100%-cash-deposit financing rule on cosmetics imports — a rule that converts each container into a pre-funded TND commitment at 7% policy-rate money for the distributor. The entry design must therefore be small-lot, fast-turn, para-channel-first: high-velocity SKUs in modest consignments that a mid-size distributor can self-fund, rather than the big-container economics every other B4 market rewards (strategist read).

The halal flip deserves its full statement, because it is the batch's cleanest positioning inversion. Morocco made halal a customs mandate; Algeria treats it as a hygiene-factor certificate; Tunisia — the Maghreb's most secular society, whose 2022 constitution removed Islam as the state religion — makes halal a quiet nice-to-have at most. The working rule for every category deck: certification travels with the GCC-origin stock anyway and costs nothing to keep, but halal never appears in Tunisian creative — the French-forward, science-and-nature register does the work, and a religious frame would read as tone-deaf to the coastal mainstream while adding nothing with the observant minority [!] consumer validation queued — state secularism ≠ proven consumer indifference (strategist read; the first market in the MENA batches where the halal card is deliberately pocketed).

The counterfeit economy deserves the deck's longest consumer paragraph, because it defines the position. OTIC's claim that over 80% of cosmetics sold in Tunisia are counterfeit — paired with the INC's finding that 77% of Tunisians CONSCIOUSLY buy fakes on affordability grounds — describes a market where the fake is not a deception but a CHOICE: the shopper knows, and the genuine article has priced itself out of her respect. That reframes the entrant's job entirely. The enemy is not the counterfeiter's deceit (she isn't deceived) but the authentic tier's arrogance (prices that made fakes rational). "Affordable authentic" — a genuine brand at a price that makes the fake's discount trivial — is therefore not a slogan here but the whole strategy: Agadir-lane 0% economics land Dabur SKUs at honest prices the French-import tier structurally cannot match, and the أصلي/authentique verification layer (scratch-QR, official-seller flags across the para mesh) gives the conscious chooser a reason to switch that costs her almost nothing. Tunisia is the programme's richest test of a thesis older than the batch: authenticity wins when it stops being a luxury (strategist read).

What the legacy actually teaches (beyond the gate). Dabur Tunisie's history is worth reading as a case study, not just a liability. It was a raw-materials trading post (Sfax imports, herbal/plant-extract classification), never a consumer-brand operation — which means its failure teaches nothing about Tunisian demand for Vatika or Herbolene; the brands were never tried. Its liquidation stall teaches a great deal about the STATE: nine years to close a ₹1-crore-net-worth shell, with the Central Bank as the named bottleneck, is the single most concrete datum the programme holds on Maghreb administrative friction — worth more than any index ranking. And its very existence sets an internal precedent: Dabur has already once judged Tunisia worth an entity, at the wrong scale and the wrong model. The asset-light re-entry this deck prescribes is not timidity; it is the corrected second draft of a thesis the company already believed (strategist read; the internal-history read is inference from public filings — Dabur's own institutional memory should overwrite it where richer).

DEEP THINK — second-order moves, scenarios & risk board

The three-clock problem (strategist read): Tunisia runs three clocks at different speeds. The debt clock (Eurobond July 2026, reserves ~106 days) could tighten import rules further within quarters — arguing for speed and small commitments. The legal clock (the liquidation's June-2028 target) argues for patience and zero new entity exposure. The competitive clock barely ticks — no rival is racing for the herbal-value seat in a 12.4M market.
