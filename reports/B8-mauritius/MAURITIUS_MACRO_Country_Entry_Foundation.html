M1 · Demography — a little India that voted itself into the Indian Ocean

Mauritius holds 1.235M people (2022 census; ~1.26M current estimates) — the programme's smallest market by headcount. Its structure is the programme's most extraordinary: Indo-Mauritians ~68% (descendants of 1834–1900s indentured migration from Bihar, UP, Tamil and Telugu country, and Gujarat), Creole ~27%, Sino- and Franco-Mauritian ~5% — with the caveat carried honestly that Mauritius has published no official ethnicity census since 1972; all modern shares are analyst estimates [!] methodology note. Religion (2022 census, official): Hindu 47.8%, Christian 32.3%, Muslim 18.2% — the only Hindu-plurality nation in Africa and third-highest Hindu share on earth after Nepal and India. Languages invert every batch assumption: English is administrative only; French dominates media and advertising; Mauritian Creole (Morisien) is the street and home language; Bhojpuri (~5% speakers) survives in ritual contexts — heritage warmth, not ad copy. Urbanisation is a diffuse 40.9% — a village-town lattice, not a metro market: wide small-format and pharmacy distribution beats any flagship-store strategy. Income: GDP/capita ~US$12k, upper-middle and high-income-adjacent — the richest per-capita market in the programme's African batches.

M3 · Trade — the batch's only tariff preference, claimable only by formal trade

CECPA (India–Mauritius Comprehensive Economic Cooperation and Partnership Agreement, in force 1 April 2021) grants Mauritius-side preference on 615 tariff lines (376 duty-free, 127 reduced, 112 quota) — commentary names chemicals/cosmetics/soaps among covered sectors, with the exact HS 3304/3305/3306 schedule unconfirmed in public sources [!] pull MRA tariff annex — W0 regulatory task. The general regime: duty bands 0/5/10/15/30% on CIF, then 15% VAT on the duty-inclusive value. The strategic hook is sharper than the arithmetic: CECPA preference applies only to properly documented direct India-origin trade — and Dabur's current Mauritius presence is, on all visible evidence, grey re-export and cross-border marketplace flow (Ubuy, CosmoStore, diaspora grocers), which claims no preference at all. Formalisation is not compliance housekeeping here; it is the margin unlock. Port Louis (497k TEU, +5.8% YoY) plus the Freeport zone offer a re-export footnote (Réunion, Seychelles, East Africa) worth a paragraph, not a strategy — RAK remains the regional hub; Mauritius is a market, secondarily a node.

M4 · Regulation — Ayurveda in statute, halal by JUM, food-vs-drug by classification

The regulatory headline outranks every other market in the programme: Mauritius recognises Ayurveda in LAW — the Ayurvedic and Other Traditional Medicines Act 1989 (with homeopathy and Chinese medicine), a Traditional Medicine Board under the Pharmacy Board registering practitioners and certifying products, integration into the PUBLIC health system since 1998, six government Ayurvedic hospitals/units plus seven clinics (Grand Bois added July 2024), an India–Mauritius traditional-medicine MoU (2015, renewed), an AYUSH Chair, the first Jan Aushadhi Kendra outside India, and an announced AYUSH Centre of Excellence at Côte d'Or inside the India-supported 500-bed SSRN hospital project [!] timeline verify. Where South Africa NAMES Ayurveda as a discipline, Mauritius OPERATES it as public infrastructure. The practical seams: Ayurvedic-medicine imports run through the Pharmacy Board's Ayurvedic committee (fees unpublished [!] direct query); retail/OTC sale of Ayurvedic products is loosely controlled (the 2016 outlet census found 16 organised Ayurvedic outlets, half selling OTC without consultation); and food/nutraceutical SKUs (Chyawanprash, honey) route via general food-import clearance — a far lighter lift than the drug path. Halal certification runs through Jamiat-Ul-Ulama of Mauritius (JUM, Port Louis; covers cosmetics and medical products; fee ~Rs200 [!] current schedule) — a meaningful shelf lever for the 18.2% Muslim population, not the table-stakes it was in the GCC. Price control exists (Consumer Protection Act 1998; 2021 Maximum Price Regulations) but names pharmaceuticals and infant formula — toothpaste and hair oil appear UNCONTROLLED on current evidence [!] confirm against the gazette schedule + MOPRI basket — a wrong assumption here has direct P&L consequences.

M5 · Health & culture — the world's #12 diabetes burden beside the world's biggest Hindu pilgrimage outside India

Mauritius carries 20.1% adult diabetes prevalence (2024) — the world's #12 burden, the highest in its IDF grouping, with nearly one in two adults over 50 diabetic and 31.7% of diabetics poorly controlled; obesity runs 36.2% (2021 NCD survey; women 41.6%) and NCDs consume ~70% of the health budget, prompting a WHO-backed 2025–2030 obesity roadmap. The sugar-free-first doctrine therefore arrives at its demographically purest case — and with its sternest warning: classic Chyawanprash is a sugar-based format, and honey-for-diabetes messaging is a compliance and ethics landmine in a country this saturated with the disease. The lead is the sugar-free variant; the classic serves the ritual cohort; honey sells as food with zero therapeutic whisper. Culture supplies the retail calendar no other market has: Maha Shivaratri — 250,000–400,000 pilgrims walking to Ganga Talao, the largest Hindu pilgrimage outside India (Feb/Mar), with government-and-Sabha-organised route support (commercial activation UNTESTED — treated as a research question, not a plan [!] field study); Divali — confirmed mall festive markets and a live gifting economy (the hamper is the entry's most natural first activation); and Cavadee (Tamil community). The Ayurvedic tradition is not memory here — it is six public clinics treating hair-fall, skin and metabolic complaints with the very vocabulary Dabur's packs speak.

The consumer decode in one story

A Sunday in Triolet. In a courtyard house three generations deep, a grandmother oils her granddaughter's hair before the week begins — the champi her own grandmother brought from Bihar in living memory of indenture, practised without a word of marketing ever having asked her to. The oil is a no-name coconut pressing from the bazaar; the Amla bottle her cousin orders "from the internet" arrives in three weeks at nearly double what her relatives in Chennai pay, so it is saved for special occasions like a perfume. Down the road, her son-in-law — 52, diagnosed at the last NCD screening like a fifth of the island — walks past the MedActiv where a French sunscreen costs Rs 899 and wonders, in Creole, why the wellness of his own tradition is the only thing the pharmacy doesn't stock. On Divali week, the family will assemble hampers — sweets, a good bottle, something for the elders — from whatever the shops offer, which this year, again, will not include the brands that would have made the hamper feel like home.

Read together: the ritual is alive and unmarketed; the willing premium is being paid to couriers instead of shelves; the state-legitimised wellness tradition is absent from the very pharmacy channel built to carry it; and the year's biggest gifting moment goes unserved by the obvious brand. Nothing in this file invents demand. Every wave in T12 is this courtyard, answered.

The competitive field's most useful single fact is its price geometry: a mass supermarket tier whose ladders are unbuilt (the 403 wall), a French pharmacy-derma tier priced at Rs 425–1,199 and thriving, and between them an empty middle where an Ayurvedic-heritage range at fair formal prices would land. DEEP THINK — is a 1.26M-person market worth five reports and an entry? The honest arithmetic: even heroic execution yields a business measured in single-digit millions of dollars — a rounding error against Saudi. Three arguments still clear the bar, and the deck stakes them explicitly. (1) EFFORT-TO-WIN RATIO: this is the programme's easiest market — mainstream ritual fit, statutory Ayurveda, a tariff preference, no organised Indian rival, price-visible grey demand already proving willingness to pay ABOVE fair retail. The entry is one distributor appointment plus one container mix; the fixed cost of "five reports" is the most expensive thing about it. (2) THE FLAGSHIP ARGUMENT: Mauritius is where India's government is building its Indian-Ocean Ayurveda showcase (AYUSH CoE, Jan Aushadhi, G2G MoUs). Dabur being ABSENT from the shelf of the world's most institutionally Ayurvedic country outside India is a reputational hole; being present, well-priced and respectful is cheap flag-planting with real diplomatic adjacency. (3) THE LEARNING ARGUMENT: nowhere else can the programme observe its products selling to an INDIAN-MAJORITY population under non-Indian retail conditions — a natural experiment on which equities travel by heritage versus which needed India's media machine. Kill-test: if the distributor conversations (Innodis-tier) yield no partner at economics that clear a modest hurdle, the market stays a managed-export line (formalised marketplace storefronts, festival gifting drops) and the five reports stand as the file for WHEN conditions change — a respectable outcome the bet prices in.
DEEP STRAT — the one-container strategy. What this market teaches the batch

Three exports. First, the FORMALISATION template: the grey-mirror table (T3b) is a reusable analytical form for every market where marketplace flow precedes distribution — Zambia, Mozambique and Namibia will each get the same three-row test. Second, the EASE-CASE discipline: Mauritius forces the programme to argue an entry on effort-to-win and strategic dividends rather than market size, and to put a small honest number in the bet instead of inflating the prize — a template for every sub-2M market the programme will ever file. Third, the treaty lesson: trade preferences are only as real as the documentation chain that claims them — a sentence the Batch-1 SE-Asia files (AITIGA) and any future Africa-India corridor work will reuse verbatim.

Reading the file as one sentence

Compressed: the market that needs the least persuading of anything Dabur has ever made currently buys it through the worst possible channel — appoint one importer, claim the treaty the grey market cannot, land one well-mixed container before Divali, and let the world's most institutionally Ayurvedic island outside India do the rest. The refusals: no size-case theatre (the ease case is the honest case); no terroir cosplay; no diabetes-honey claims; no Bhojpuri copy; no five-year plan.

One sizing honesty before the calibration: the bet's revenue clause (~US$650k/yr) would be an embarrassment in any other file in this programme, and stating it plainly is the point. The programme's credibility across 42 countries depends on its willingness to size small markets small — a file that inflated Mauritius to justify its own page count would quietly corrupt every larger file's numbers too. The dividends that justify the entry beyond the P&L line — the flagship presence in the world's most Ayurvedic jurisdiction outside India, the natural experiment on heritage-equity travel, the formalisation template for three sibling markets — are named, dated and inspectable in the bet rather than waved at.

And one operational note for the regional office: Mauritius should report into whichever desk owns the Indian-Ocean lane, not into the Africa cluster's volume logic — its KPIs (price-fairness gap, hamper seasons, flagship dividends) would read as failure on a Nigeria dashboard and as exactly-on-plan on their own. Small markets die in big markets' spreadsheets; the file's last governance ask is a reporting line that lets this one live.

Calibration note

Strong ground: the institutional facts (the 1989 Act, the clinics, CECPA, the census religion data, the G2G machinery — all primary-grade), the demand signals (grey-market listings, India's #2 honey-supplier position, the diabetes burden), and the channel map (chain counts from company/press sources). Deliberately humble ground: this is the programme's thinnest price evidence — ONE captured supermarket price — and the macro says so rather than inventing ladders; the ethnicity shares carry the 1972-census caveat; the CECPA HS schedule, price-control scope, Pharmacy Board fees and distributor identities are all W0 confirmations; and the GAP RULE is triggered in full — no regional expert exists in the programme's routing, so French/Creole research prompts are emitted verbatim in the appendix and no vernacular judgement is faked anywhere. No confidential Dabur data was requested, received, or used.

The bet's shape, for the board. Every clause is sized to the island: the partner clause accepts a documented fallback as success (the file's honesty about scale extends to its exits); the price clause is the entry's moral core — beating the grey ceiling by ≥15% is what "homecoming" means in rupees; the zero-claims clause is absolute in a country where a fifth of adults carry the diagnosis the category's oldest format contains sugar for; and the revenue clause is deliberately, visibly small. The dividends clause makes the flagship-and-learning arguments inspectable: if by 2029 the programme cannot point to documented learning from watching its equities sell to an Indian-majority population without India's media machine, that argument was decoration, and the post-mortem says so.
