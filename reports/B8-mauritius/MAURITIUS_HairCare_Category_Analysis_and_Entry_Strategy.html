C1 · Category frame — a ritual without a shelf

The structural fact outranks every number: with ~68% of the island Indo-Mauritian, champi — the oil massage — is a mainstream household practice transplanted whole from Bihar, UP and Tamil country in the 1830s–1900s and never interrupted. This is not Qatar's diaspora aisle or South Africa's ingredient-education project; the ritual is indigenous here, multi-generational, and pre-sold. The numbers that exist: hair-care imports heading to ~US$15.4M by 2026 (~2.2% CAGR) directional, trade-data-service estimate [!]; cosmetics overall ~US$104M by 2026; category growth cited at 12–13%/yr [!] vendor-report figure; e-commerce "care products" already 7.8% of a US$353M online market. The supply side is the anomaly: the ritual is served by unbranded bazaar coconut pressings at the value end and courier-priced grey imports (Ubuy, CosmoStore-from-Europe, diaspora webshops) at the branded end — with no formal branded shelf between them. One supermarket price was capturable on the whole island (Creme of Nature Curl Activator, Rs 475/449 at Winners) — a texture-care import anchor that proves three-digit rupee price points clear in mass retail.

C2 · Demand architecture — three oils, one island

Engine one: the champi household (the mainstream). Weekly oiling across generations, currently buying bazaar coconut oil for routine use and treating branded Amla as an occasional luxury because grey pricing made it one. The job: return the brand to everyday status at fair formal prices — the macro's grey-ceiling arithmetic says formal retail can beat courier prices by ≥15% while earning more margin. Engine two: the ingredient-conscious upgrader. The same global routine-building current runs here (the one captured price is a curl activator, not an oil), Facebook-first, French/Creole-speaking; Vatika's enriched formats (amla+henna, hibiscus-adjacent) serve the upgrade without leaving the ritual. Engine three: the Creole texture segment. Real, organised at salon level, keratin-leaning — served respectfully with function-first framing (pre-poo oil, moisture sealing) in Creole-first voice, never with a transplanted champi narrative. The premium French tier keeps its own customers; the deck does not visit.

The consumer decode in one story

Sunday morning in Triolet, again — the macro's courtyard, now watched through the category's eyes. The grandmother warms the oil between her palms; it is bazaar coconut, decanted into an old Amla bottle whose label has half worn away, because the bottle itself outlived the affordable supply of what it once held. Her daughter, 41, a nurse at Victoria Hospital, orders the real thing twice a year through her cousin's Ubuy account — Rs [the number this deck cannot yet print] for 300ml plus a three-week wait — and rations it for special occasions: before weddings, before Divali, before the school prize-giving. Her own daughter, 19, watches French get-ready-with-me videos and has begun asking whether the family oil "has anything in it" — the enriched-format question arriving by Facebook. Three generations, one ritual, three unmet needs: the grandmother needs consistency at bazaar honesty, the mother needs her everyday brand returned to everyday prices, the daughter needs the upgrade tier to exist on an island shelf at all.

The deck's whole architecture is this courtyard: R1/R2 return the worn-label bottle to daily life at fair prices; R3's enriched Vatika answers the daughter's question; and the fairness launch tells the nurse, publicly and once, that the courier years are over. Nothing needs converting. Everything needs stocking.

C3 · Brand-by-brand teardown

3.0 Self-teardown: a luxury by accident

Dabur Amla 300ml sells at a Mauritian diaspora webshop; Vatika ships from European warehouses via CosmoStore and rides Ubuy's cross-border rails. Every route adds courier economics to a value brand, converting an everyday Indian household SKU into an occasional imported treat — premiumisation by neglect, the exact inverse of strategy. No official distributor exists; no CECPA preference is claimed; no shelf presence anywhere in ~50 organised supermarkets. The teardown sentence: the brand's Mauritius problem is that its most loyal market in Africa pays the most per millilitre for it — and the fix is one appointment and one container.

3.1 The bazaar coconut pressing — the real incumbent

The volume incumbent is unbranded: artisanal coconut oil from local pressing traditions, bought at markets and shops at commodity prices. Its strengths are trust, price and cultural legitimacy; its weaknesses are inconsistency, no quality assurance, and zero format innovation. The lesson is postural: never position against the tradition — position as its reliable form. Branded oil wins on consistency, purity assurance and enriched variants, priced above the pressing and below the grey ceiling, with the pressing treated in comms the way Bu Saif and Taif were treated in the Gulf files: honoured, not fought.

3.2 Parachute (Marico) — the rival that may not have shown up

No Mauritius retail evidence for Parachute surfaced despite targeted searching [!] walk confirms — remarkable for the world's branded-coconut-oil leader in an island of coconut-oil users. If confirmed absent, the first-organised-mover window in branded hair oil is OPEN in the single most culturally primed market on earth outside South Asia. If present-but-thin, the contest is winnable on range breadth (Amla + enriched Vatika vs single-note coconut). Either way the window is measured in ranging cycles, not years — Marico reads the same censuses.

3.3 The French premium tier — the ceiling that minds its own business

L'Oréal (via MedActiv pharmacies and MADO's boutique), Klorane and the salon trade own a genuine premium tier at pharmacy price points (the LRP anchors: Rs 425–1,199 across skincare). It does not compete for the champi ritual and will not respond to a formalised Amla entry. Its value to this deck is calibration: the island demonstrably pays French-pharmacy prices for hair-and-skin credibility — which prices Vatika's enriched tier comfortably into Rs 200–350 without blinking.

3.4 The texture-care imports — the segment's servers

The one captured price on the island is a US texture-care brand at Rs 475 in Winners — proof the Creole segment is served by imports at premium prices, thinly. Dabur's coconut/argan-adjacent oils can serve the pre-poo and sealing steps of that routine at half the imported price point — an adjacency entered in Creole-first voice with local stylist partnerships panel + stylist study, never by re-labelling the champi story.

The competitive quiet, read correctly

A deck this confident about an uncontested lane owes the board a paragraph on why the lane is uncontested. Three honest hypotheses. One: the market is genuinely too small for multinational attention — 1.26M people is below the threshold at which Marico or Emami staffs a country manager, and the grey flow quietly serves the demand they'd otherwise chase (the most likely answer, and the one that makes the window durable). Two: someone tried and failed quietly — an importer relationship that lapsed, a container that didn't turn — in which case the distributor interviews will surface the history and its lessons before the appointment signs [!] interview question #1. Three: the demand is thinner than the structural logic implies — the ritual persisting on bazaar oil precisely BECAUSE branded oil never mattered enough to fight for. The deck weights the first hypothesis, plans for the second, and lets the bet's ritual-conversion gate carry the third honestly. What it refuses to do is treat quiet as proof of treasure: quiet is a question the walk and the interviews answer.

The channel doctrine in one paragraph

The channel plan mirrors the island's own shape: one Asian-import natural home (Way) that says yes fast and proves the range; one household default (Winners) that converts proof into national normality; a shop-and-tabagie lattice that carries the diffuse 59% the chains never reach — reachable only through the distributor's van-sales muscle, which is why the appointment interviews weigh rural reach as heavily as chain relationships [!] interview criterion; a pharmacy tier for the enriched formats' credibility; and official e-storefronts whose real job is displacing the grey pages that currently define the brand's digital shelf. The French-format chains come last not because they matter least but because they need the most proof — and on an island where every buyer knows every other buyer, proof travels at dinner-party speed. One market, one buying community, one reputation: the channel doctrine is simply to build it in the right order.

Reading the ladder against the grey ceiling

A worked example makes T5's logic concrete. Suppose the W0 browse finds the grey Amla 300ml landing at MUR 320–380 through the courier channels (the capture that anchors everything). The R2 target of 220–280 then does three jobs at once: it undercuts the worst grey price by ~35% and the best by ~15% (the fairness clause's floor), it sits roughly 2.5–3× the bazaar pressing's per-use cost (a premium the consistency-and-purity story carries comfortably, on the evidence of what households already pay couriers), and it leaves the R3 enriched tier a clean 40–80 rupee step for the daughter's upgrade question. If instead the browse finds grey prices LOWER than modelled — couriers competing harder than the listings suggest — the whole ladder shifts down a notch and the CECPA line absorbs the compression; that resilience, not any single number, is what the authored architecture is actually for. The ladder is a set of RELATIONSHIPS (floor multiple, ceiling discount, rung steps) wearing provisional numbers; the browse changes the numbers, never the relationships.

C11 · The two flag-moves, argued

DEEP THINK — the fairness launch: spending an unrepeatable story correctly. Formalisation hands this entry something no marketing budget can buy twice: the day the brand arrives, prices measurably FALL versus the grey ceiling every loyal household has been paying. Handled well, that is a homecoming narrative with receipts — earned media, community goodwill, and a price-politics climate suddenly on the brand's side. Handled lazily, it is a week-one price promotion nobody remembers. The design: publish the comparison honestly (grey landed price vs formal shelf price, named SKUs), let community voices carry it on Facebook, anchor it to the fairness register the island's medicine-price anger has primed — and never repeat it, because a fairness story retold becomes an ad. Second-order: the grey channel's re-listers lose their economics overnight on matched SKUs — the cleanest grey-displacement mechanism is making the grey price embarrassing. Kill-test: if the CECPA annex disappoints (HS lines excluded) and formal landed costs cannot beat the grey ceiling by ≥15%, the fairness launch is OFF — the entry proceeds on availability alone and the bet's price clause converts to availability metrics, minuted.
DEEP STRAT — the window sprint: Parachute's absence as a clock. If the walk confirms no organised Marico presence, the sequence compresses: (1) the distributor appointment names hair as container #1's anchor category; (2) Way listing within the first quarter (the Asian-import home needs no persuading); (3) Winners inside two quarters with the fairness story as the trade pitch's centrepiece; (4) the bazaar-respect comms run from day one so the tradition reads the brand as its ally before any rival arrives to frame it otherwise; (5) enriched Vatika follows the Amla beachhead, not beside it — one story at a time on an island this size. Works if: the window is real and the distributor moves at island speed (fast — one buying community). Fails if: Marico reads the same file and moves first with Parachute's simpler story. Kill-switch: if outflanked, pivot from "the ritual's brand comes home" to range superiority (Amla + enriched vs plain coconut) — a weaker story the deck prices as the downside case.

What this deck teaches the batch

Two exports. C16 · What does not transplant

Three stay-homes, one inversion, one pure case. South Africa's ingredient claim-back war stays home — nobody here needs teaching what amla is; the fight never existed. The GCC's two-register weighting machinery stays home in its usual form — there is no expat/national split to weight; the registers here are generational and ethnic-cohort, simpler and gentler. South Africa's braid-care column has only a faint echo (the texture adjacency is a pilot, not a pillar). The inversion: everywhere else the programme built demand toward distribution; here distribution is the ONLY missing element of a demand system that has run for 190 years — the purest formalisation case the programme will ever file, which is exactly why its lessons (the grey-mirror table, the fairness launch, the treaty-documentation discipline) were exported to the batch via the macro before this deck was even written.
