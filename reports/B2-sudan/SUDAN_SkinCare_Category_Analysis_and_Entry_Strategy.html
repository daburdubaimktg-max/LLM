WATCH-FRAME (binding — read with the macro). Sudan is in the fourth year of civil war (SAF vs RSF, since April 2023). The country-level verdict is WATCH + pre-position for recovery, not a live launch; this deck is a recovery-window readiness assessment. And skin care carries a category-specific go/no-go the others do not: Sudan has imposed import bans on "luxury/unnecessary" goods that explicitly include cosmetics and perfumes [Dabanga] — so an HS-3304 shipment could be blocked outright [!] re-validate the current banned list before any shipment. The demand substrate is intact and deeply ritualised; the supply/channel/payment machinery is war-broken; and the macro's reputation/FX gates apply in full.
THE ASK: Sudanese skin care is organised around a living, female-led ritual complex — dukhan (دخان, the acacia/talh smoke bath, a 40-day bridal regimen that "evens skin colour" to a prized golden glow), dilka (دلكة, a turmeric- sandalwood-clove-flour scrub that exfoliates and evens tone), khumra (خمرة, blended perfume paste) and karkar, collectively the bridal kabarayt. The native codes — turmeric, honey, sandalwood, acacia, clove, rose, musk — map almost 1:1 onto Dabur's herbal repertoire. The category's centre of gravity, though, is a crux Dabur must confront and avoid: skin-lightening is enormous but toxic — ~85.8% of surveyed Sudanese women use lightening products (74.6% topical creams), with hydroquinone measured at 0–5.75% and mercury/steroid creams (Faiza, Caro White, Dermovate) circulating in a grey, unregulated, reputationally radioactive trade rooted in colourism. Dabur's call is unambiguous: skip the bleach war and own the third space — "natural glow · even-tone · dilka-turmeric-congruent — NEVER bleach," which is culturally native (dukhan/ dilka already claim glow and even-tone, not chemical whitening), Islamically resonant (natural beauty; the fiqh principle against self-harm), and on the right side of regulatory drift — a positioning a real Sudanese brand (Sukar Cosmetics, "#BeautifulAsYouAre") has already proven. The hero is Dabur Gulabari (rose water) — a pre-legitimised Islamic code (the Ka'aba is washed with rose water; ablution/hammam use) needing no education — supported by DermoViva (the turmeric even-tone "dilka in a tube," with the "Fairness" wording dropped). Quarantine Fem (a bleach/fairness brand — reputationally toxic here). Frame it "natural herbal halal," never "Ayurveda." Recovery-window verdict (macro T14): BUILD. Halal is the total baseline; the sachet is non-negotiable (glass rose water lands at 4–7× India price).

C1 — The ask (executive summary)

Macro anchor (§M6, §M11, T14): Skin is ranked BUILD for the recovery window — enter on Gulabari naturals + the dukhan/dilka ritual, avoid the whitening war. Enter via the RAK → Port Sudan lane (the UAE is Sudan's #1 cosmetics supplier ~40%) through the attara + salon + dukkan triad; halal-total; USD/hawala; sachet-first; explicitly non-bleach — but gate everything on the cosmetics import-ban check.

The play in one screen. Three truths make skin a recovery-window build. First, the winnable lane is the one Dabur is culturally native to, and it is the safe one: the lightening category is huge (~86%) but toxic, unregulated, grey and turning reputationally against itself, while the natural/heritage/halal pole — the dukhan/dilka/khumra ritual, rose water, turmeric, oils — is pre-loaded in Sudanese beauty and uncontested by a branded modern player. Dabur should own "natural glow, even-tone, never bleach," not field a fairness SKU. Second, the hero asset needs no education: Dabur Gulabari rose water is a shared Islamic code (the Ka'aba is washed with rose water; it is used in ablution and the hammam), a dilka mixing agent, already Gulf-recognised and halal — so Dabur allies with the culture's own even-tone ritual rather than fighting it, and DermoViva's turmeric even-tone is literally "dilka in a tube" once the "Fairness" wording is stripped. Third, the benefit concentrates on exposed skin and the sachet is the format: covering culture + a hot/dry/dusty climate push the daily job onto face, hands, feet (hydration, glow, dust-defence) plus the private bridal ritual layer — and the war economy makes single-use/small packs non-negotiable (a 120ml glass rose water lands at 4–7× India MRP). The move is a WATCH-window pre-position, not a launch: (1) resolve the cosmetics import-ban status (the go/no-go); (2) secure 1–2 Port Sudan importers and design a sachet/PET-first Gulabari + de-fairnessed DermoViva range; (3) seed the salon/bridal + diaspora-creator economy on an explicitly non-bleach platform. Frame "natural herbal halal," never "Ayurveda." Do not lead with Fem or any fairness claim, do not import glass bottles at entry, and do not feed the colourism demand. The LEAP lesson (C13): own the "natural-glow × halal × never-bleach" intersection, in real Sudanese Arabic (naḍāra, not tabyīḍ). Reputation Bet (C18): when the recovery window opens (and the import ban lifts), Gulabari is the branded natural-glow/rose-water reference across the attara + salon + dukkan triad, sachet-led, explicitly non-bleach — or the safe-glow space is taken first (a state ban tightening, or a Nivea/Glow&Lovely "natural" pivot) and Dabur stays absent.

Rail 2 — the whitening crux, confronted. The category's centre of gravity is skin-lightening, and Dabur's decision to avoid it is evidence-backed. Prevalence is ~85.8% of surveyed women (74.6% topical creams; heaviest 20–29), with measured hydroquinone 0–5.75% (local) / 0–3.21% (imported), F&L samples 0.47–4.76%, documented redness/sun-sensitivity/scarring, and mercury/steroid (clobetasol) creams in circulation — a documented public-health harm rooted in a colonial-era colourism. Sudan has no enforced cosmetic-lightening ban (unlike South Sudan's 2019 DFCA ban), but a recovering, health-sector-rebuilding Sudan is more likely to tighten than loosen — so a "never bleach / clinically clean / halal- natural" brand is on the right side of regulatory drift. A real Sudanese brand has already proven the space: Sukar Cosmetics ("#BeautifulAsYouAre") uses natural-beauty to discourage bleaching.

So what: the lightening category is huge, toxic, grey, unregulated and reputationally turning. Dabur should skip it entirely and own the contrast — tone-evenness + radiance + skin-health (naḍāra / ishrāqa ṭabīʿiyya), never whitening (tabyīḍ / tafṭīḥ) — which is honest, Islamically resonant, and exactly what the dukhan/dilka ritual already claims. Note honestly: the anti-bleach Islamic frame is credible but must be validated in Sudanese dialect before airing.

The diaspora is proving both halves of the thesis. Two diaspora signals validate the recovery-window bet. First, heritage beauty commands a premium: Sudanese khumra and dilka sell at $18–45 a set on Etsy and TikTok Shop to a global Sudanese audience, and diaspora creators like Shahd Batal (300k+ subscribers, dilka/dukhan/karkar content) have turned the ritual into aspirational, exportable culture — proving willingness-to-pay for exactly the natural-heritage codes Dabur would carry. Second, the anti-bleach lane is commercially real: Sukar Cosmetics, a Sudanese natural-skincare brand founded by a certified pharmacist, has built its identity on "#BeautifulAsYouAre" — an explicit, branded refusal of skin- bleaching that is winning an audience. Together these say the natural-glow-not-bleach positioning is not a strategist's hope but a proven, funded consumer movement — and that no FMCG major is yet in it. Strategist read (opinion): Dabur's job is to be the scaled, halal- certified, consistent-quality expression of a movement that artisanal brands and diaspora creators have already de-risked — the modern complement to the ritual, on the safe side of the bleach war.

Payment, the import ban, and the two go/no-go gates. Two gates make or break the skin build, and the second is unique to this category. The payment/FX gate is the same war-economy stack — no LCs, USD/hawala settlement, a 10–50% cash-out drag, and a parallel-FX penalty of +25–60% because the importer must buy dollars on the black market to pay the FOB — which, stacked on a 40–100% cosmetics duty and 17% VAT, is exactly what drives the 4–7× India-MRP landed cost. The import-ban gate is the sharper one: Sudan has placed cosmetics, perfumes and bath soap on a "luxury/ unnecessary" import-ban list [Dabanga], so an HS-3304 shipment could be blocked outright regardless of demand or registration — making the ban status a hard go/no-go that must be resolved before any shipment planning, and watched as closely as the ceasefire itself. The strategic consequence is that skin, uniquely among Sudan's four categories, has a regulatory switch that can flip the whole thesis off independent of the war — so the pre-position work (importer, sachet range, salon/creator seeding, dialect validation) is deliberately low-cost and non-committal until that switch flips to "allowed."
