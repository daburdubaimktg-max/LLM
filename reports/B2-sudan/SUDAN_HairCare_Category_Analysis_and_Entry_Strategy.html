WATCH-FRAME (binding — read with the macro). Sudan is in the fourth year of civil war (SAF vs RSF, since April 2023). The country-level verdict is WATCH + pre-position for recovery, not a live launch — and this category deck is a recovery-window readiness assessment, not a go-now plan. The demand substrate is intact and arguably deepened (the hair-oiling ritual, the bridal beauty complex, Amla brand memory, diaspora pride); the supply/channel/payment machinery is war-broken (modern retail looted, e-commerce near-zero, currency collapsed, Port Sudan the only functioning lane). Every "first move" below is a no-regret pre-position move, and the reputation/FX gates from the macro (the UAE-RSF conflict-financing narrative; SDN screening; hard-currency settlement) apply in full.
THE ASK: Hair is Sudan's strongest recovery-window right-to-win — because the ritual is already Dabur's, performed with a home-cooked oil, not a brand. The cultural spine is karkar (كركار) — a sesame-oil base cooked with beeswax, rendered tallow (wadak, ودك) and aromatics — used for centuries for long, retained, strong hair, the northern-Sudanese beauty ideal of waist-length mushat (مشاط) braids. It sits inside the 40-day bridal complex karkar → dukhan (دخان) → dilka (دلكة), collectively kabarayt (كبريت) — a war-resilient, female-led, WoM-propagated economy — and its mental model is retention, not runway shine (the same "length-by-cutting-breakage" logic that chebe owns). No FMCG major has claimed this ritual; it is owned by informal makers and a booming diaspora karkar/chebe e-com (Chebeauty $12/4oz, Miss Sahel, Uhuru). Dabur's arsenal maps onto it 1:1: Dabur Amla = the long/strong/dark equity, Vatika (coconut + amla + henna + shikakai) = the branded, shelf-stable karkar-analogue, Vatika Black-Seed (habbat-ul-baraka, حبة البركة, Turkey-sourced) = the highest Islamic-resonance SKU, and Vatika Henna = the hinna lock — all UAE-made and halal-marketed. Frame it "natural herbal halal oil for long, well-rooted hair," never "Ayurveda." Recovery-window verdict (macro T14): LEAD. Halal is the total baseline; the covered-hair reframe (scalp/root/retention under hijab) is the creative key.

C1 — The ask (executive summary)

Macro anchor (§M6, §M11, T14): Hair is ranked LEAD for the recovery window — Vatika/Amla are established Gulf hero franchises flowing off the RAK hub, mapping 1:1 onto the karkar oiling ritual, with the covered-hair reframe as the creative key. The play in one screen. Three truths make hair Sudan's lead recovery bet. First, the ritual is already Dabur's — but performed with a home-cooked oil, not a brand: karkar cleanses/nourishes, chebe/karkar retain length, mushat braids display it, henna colours it, and each has a near-perfect Dabur overlay (Vatika coconut oil, Dabur Amla, Vatika Henna). So the recovery-window job is demand-capture, not demand-creation — offer the branded, hygienic, consistent, halal version of a ritual the consumer already performs, honouring karkar by name, never replacing it. Second, the benefit ladder is covered-hair, not runway: ~97% Muslim, most adult women cover in public, so the felt problems are scalp health, root strength, length retention and anti-breakage under the scarf — a ladder the shine-led Sunsilk/Pantene imports never speak to, and the one Amla's "long, strong, dark" equity and Vatika's multi-herb nourishment answer directly. Third, no FMCG major owns the karkar/chebe territory — it is held by informal makers and a diaspora e-com boom that proves willingness-to-pay a premium for Sudanese hair heritage ($12–15 artisan karkar), leaving the branded-mass lane wide open. The move is a WATCH-window pre-position, not a launch: (1) secure one trusted, FX-capable, SDN-clean Port Sudan importer settled via hawala; (2) design a sachet-led, USD-anchored Amla/Vatika portfolio (sachet the hero unit, bottle the bridal-gift SKU); (3) build heritage equity now via radio + diaspora hair-creators so Dabur is first back on the shelf. Lead Dabur Amla as the volume hero, Vatika coconut as the branded karkar-analogue co-hero, Vatika Black-Seed as the Islamic-resonance premium, Vatika Henna as the hinna lock; settle in USD/AED via hawala; win on halal + herbal + karkar-legitimacy + black-seed Islamic cue stacked. Do not lead with "Ayurveda", do not transplant a shine ad, do not appropriate karkar/chebe (honour/credit it), and do not build in-country distribution while the war runs. The LEAP lesson (C13): own the "branded karkar × halal-natural × long-covered-hair" intersection, in real Sudanese Arabic. Reputation Bet (C18): when the recovery window opens, Dabur Amla + Vatika are first back on the Omdurman/Port Sudan shelf, priced/sachet-led/reordering off the RAK lane, out-indexing any Gulf/Egyptian value oil in herbal-hair salience — or the ritual stays owned by home-cooked karkar and diaspora e-com.

C2 — Category frame, size & the covered-hair structure

Sudan has no defensible standalone hair-care market figure (war has erased reliable data; "washing & cleaning preparations" imports were ~$5.35M in 2024, Egypt/China/Indonesia/Saudi/Turkey the origins [OEC 2024] — a fraction of the real informal + grey flow). So the recovery-window frame is penetration × ritual × the ~50M population, not a dollar tag. The market is ~100% imported now (war-damaged manufacturing), oil-and-natural- first, and shaped by a covering culture that redefines the benefit.

The covered-hair reframe. With hair worn under hijab most of the day, the benefit ladder shifts from public display to scalp health, root strength, length retention and anti-breakage under covering — hair is a private, family and bridal asset, oiled and braided for those contexts. The shine-led imports (Sunsilk/Pantene) structurally miss this; Amla's "long, strong, dark" equity and Vatika's multi-herb nourishment answer it directly. The population frame: ~50M people, ~97% Muslim, with a long-hair-retention ideal (waist-length mushat braids) that is identical to the mental model Amla already owns globally — a rare case where Dabur's flagship equity and the local beauty canon are the same sentence.

Why the ritual is war-resilient — and why that de-risks the recovery bet. The most important structural fact about Sudanese hair care is that its demand engine is counter-cyclical. Because karkar-making, braiding and the kabarayt bridal complex are home-cooked, communal, low-cash and tied to marriage and female identity, they do not switch off when incomes collapse — they move informal and shrink in budget, but the behaviour and the ingredient literacy persist. A displaced woman in Kassala still knows what karkar does for length-retention and still braids for a wedding. That is categorically different from a discretionary shine category a recession simply erases — and it is why the recovery-window task is demand-capture, not demand-creation: the habit, the frequency and the benefit belief all survive the war intact, waiting for a branded, hygienic, affordable, halal version to step into a ritual the consumer never stopped performing. It makes hair the lowest-demand-risk of Sudan's four categories, gated only by the war and the reputation/FX constraints, not by any doubt about whether the consumer wants the benefit.

C4 — Demand engine: the karkar / kabarayt ritual (Module — consumer truth)

The demand engine is ritual, female-led and war-resilient. Karkar (كركار) — the name from the stirring motion of making it — is a sesame-oil base cooked with beeswax, rendered tallow (wadak, ودك), cloves, sandalwood, mahlab and aromatic oils, worked into scalp and braids for long, retained, strong hair. It sits inside the multi-day pre-wedding dag al-rihah (دقّ الريحة, "pounding of the scents") and the 40-day bridal complex.

So what: Dabur does not need to create a habit, a frequency or a benefit belief — all three exist, and they are counter-cyclical (home-made, communal, tied to marriage and identity, so they survive the war). The recovery-window job is to attach an existing, beloved ritual to a branded, hygienic, halal format without desecrating it — "the karkar you know, now in a bottle, blessed by black seed."

Halal & the black-seed Islamic cue. Halal is total and effectively mandatory (~97% Muslim) — Dabur's UAE-made, halal-marketed range clears it natively, a hard advantage over any non-certified rival (the China-flip: halal is a positive/mandatory here). The sharpest faith cue is Vatika Black-Seed (habbat-ul-baraka / حبة البركة, "the seed of blessing," Turkey-sourced) — prophetic-medicine resonance no Western shampoo can credibly borrow. Amla reads as natural/herbal; both let Dabur speak a faith-and-nature language competitors cannot.

The diaspora is proving the thesis in real time. The single most useful validation of the karkar right-to-win is not an in-country statistic — it is the booming diaspora e-commerce in Sudanese hair heritage. Brands like Chebeauty, Uhuru Naturals, AMALICO and Miss Sahel sell karkar and chebe oils at $12–28 a jar to a global Sudanese and pan-African audience, and creators like Shahd Batal (300k+ YouTube subscribers, built on Sudanese hair/dilka/karkar ritual content) have turned the tradition into aspirational, exportable culture. That tells Dabur three things: the ritual has proven, premium willingness-to-pay; the heritage narrative travels and is amplified by a diaspora that funds and shapes in-country taste via remittance; and no FMCG major is in the conversation. The recovery-window opportunity is therefore twofold — an affordable, hygienic, halal mass oil (Amla/Vatika) for the in-country base that cannot afford $12 artisan karkar, and a later karkar-adjacent heritage variant that rides the same diaspora wave the artisan brands opened. Strategist read (opinion): the diaspora is not a side-market — it is the marketing department Dabur does not have to build, already telling the world that Sudanese long-hair heritage is worth paying for.

Payment & settlement (the war-economy money stack). How trade and consumers pay shapes every commercial term. On the trade side there are no letters of credit — importers settle in USD/AED through hawala networks running RAK/Jebel Ali ↔ Port Sudan, so Dabur invoices in hard currency and a distributor's hawala relationships matter as much as its shelf reach. On the consumer side, cash (SDG) is scarce and inflation-ravaged; the digital lifeline is Bankak (Bank of Khartoum's app, 50M+ transactions since the war began, +85% activations), but cash-out via agents carries ~20% surcharge and the app has suffered nationwide outages, while hawala withdrawal fees run 10–50% of value. The practical consequence is that a fixed SDG shelf price is impossible: Dabur must price in USD internally, expose small SDG sachet tickets, and expect frequent re-pricing as the parallel rate slides. Any recovery-window model must (a) settle in hard currency via a trusted Port Sudan importer, (b) build 10–20%+ cash-out friction into the margin, and (c) design a sachet ticket that survives weekly re-pricing — a discipline that reinforces the sachet-hero architecture from every angle.
