The consumer decode in one story

The Rwandan skin consumer's story pivots on 2018 in a way no other market's does. Before it, Kigali's beauty economy ran the regional pattern — a majority of urban women using lighteners, a shelf full of imported transformation promises, a practice normalised by decades. Then the head of state named the harm in public, the police moved within weeks, and the shelves changed inside a season. What the consumer learned was not only that the products were gone; she learned that her government watches this category personally — a lesson that reshaped what "safe" means at the point of purchase. Post-2018, visible legality is itself a product attribute: the S-Mark, the registered label, the brand that was never associated with the banned tier carry a reassurance premium that did not exist before the ban. Nobody has measured her post-ban behaviour (the deck's honestly-named gap), but the structure of her choice is knowable: a care-seeking majority, a black-market remnant she must now seek out deliberately at legal risk, and a legal shelf that still sells function without meaning. Herbolene's herbal story plus conspicuous formality — the most compliant-looking product on the shelf, in the market where compliance is watched from the top — is aimed at the exact psychological space the ban created. The platform's quietness is not timidity; it is fluency in the post-2018 register.

SYNTHESIS: a three-tier jelly shelf with an undefended brand tier, a value ladder owned by a Kenyan import, a banned pocket under personal presidential policing, artisans the market doesn't yet trust, and Dabur's passenger SKU already aboard through the same door as oral care. The strategy: one formalisation (Herbolene), one bridge (coconut body oil riding the hair launch), one alignment (the safe-skin ground the state already built), zero adventures.

DEEP THINK — governance as the product, tested. The skeptic's challenge: this deck spends more words on what it won't do than what it will — is that strategy or theatre? Test it against the market's specific physics. (1) The asymmetry is real: skin's upside here is US$0.3–0.6M; a single exclusion breach in the market where the head of state personally polices the category could tax the ~US$3–6M Rwanda country thesis AND echo through the EAC programme — a 10:1 downside-to-upside ratio that makes governance spend the highest-return line in the deck. (2) The false-friend incident is the proof of mechanism: uruhu was caught by process (dictionary cross-check), not luck; the negative-check panel protocol industrialises that catch for the vocabulary class where an error costs most — the lightening register. (3) The alignment posture is falsifiable: if quiet compliance were mere theatre, the bet would not stake the deck's declared success on a PERFECT audited record — it does, and pre-declares the governance failure as worse than the commercial one. Second-order: the strictest-market discipline pays forward — vocabulary blacklists, negative-check protocols and the presidential-assumption incident clause become programme assets that every remaining market (the Maghreb's own lightening regimes, West Africa's) will inherit at zero marginal cost. Rwanda is where the programme's skin governance gets stress-tested at maximum enforcement so it never fails anywhere softer.

The Sulfo dual-role, resolved in prose

The strangest table in this deck is the one where Sulfo appears twice — as the jelly floor Herbolene flanks and as the macro's leading distributor candidate — and the resolution deserves more than a row. The apparent conflict dissolves on ladder arithmetic: Sante and Malaika occupy the value floor Sulfo's own economics defend best (local manufacture, no freight); Herbolene sits two rungs up at brand-tier money with a herbal story Sulfo neither makes nor claims. A distributor margin on Herbolene earns Sulfo MORE per unit than its own floor jelly earns in manufacture — carrying the flanker is rational, not treasonous, and the proposal is made in exactly those terms. The residual risk (Sulfo learns the herbal-jelly play and formulates its own) is real but slow — formulation, registration and story-building take the same two years they'd take anyone — and the mitigation is the same speed logic as the hair deck's: by the time a Sante Herbal exists, Herbolene's formality, story and shelf position are set. If the dual-role talks fail on category-conflict grounds, the independent-distributor fallback per the macro triage costs a relationship, not the plan.

DEEP STRAT — the one-bottle bridge, engineered. The Vatika coconut body-oil bridge is the deck's only genuinely new commercial idea, and its engineering is deliberately minimal: the SAME 100ml bottle the hair deck launches as R1 appears on the skin shelf and in Kasha's body-care category with dual-use copy («for hair and body» — the cow-ghee tradition never separated them; kwisiga applies to both). One SKU, one registration, one price (1,800–2,500), two facings universes. The economics: dual-aisle placement roughly doubles the bottle's velocity potential at zero incremental supply cost, and the heritage register does the positioning work in both aisles simultaneously. The risks are two: shelf confusion (solved by shelf-talker discipline — the duka nails it to one location anyway) and category-copy drift (the panel clears ONE copy set serving both aisles; no aisle-specific claims that could diverge). Works if: consumers read oil as oil, the way their grandmothers did. Fails if: modern-trade buyers demand aisle-specific SKUs — in which case the bridge splits into two labels at W2 with the velocity data deciding whether the second registration is worth US$1,250. The bridge is the passenger model's purest expression: a product that travels on another deck's economics end to end.

Channel doctrine in prose: skin's channel plan is the country plan with two skin-specific amendments. First, the pharmacy/Goodlife shelf matters MORE for skin than its volume suggests — post-2018, the pharmacy is where the anxious skin question gets asked («is this one safe?»), and a Herbolene facing between Neutrogena and the prescription counter answers it wordlessly; the OTC deck's wellness-corner partnership therefore carries one Herbolene facing as a deliberate governance exhibit, not a volume play. Second, the artisan boutique tier is refused as a channel but embraced as a REFERENCE — the deck's quarterly sweep reads Kandaka's and Asili's pricing as the story-premium ceiling, the same observatory role Hemani plays for hair. Everything else — duka trays, Kimironko wholesale, the Kasha catalogue, the Sawa Citi contract — is the country machine doing its fourth category's work at marginal cost.

The post-ban evidence gap, handled honestly

The deck's central epidemiological fact — what Rwandan women actually do post-2018 — does not exist in public evidence, and this passage marks how the strategy handles that. Both prevalence figures (60.9% and 54.5%) are pre-ban Kigali samples; CNN's 2022 follow-up establishes persistence but not scale; and no RBC/MoH re-survey surfaced. The strategy is therefore built to be INVARIANT to the missing number: at any post-ban prevalence — 10% or 40% — the exclusion is absolute (the law, not the market, sets it), the legal range's positioning is unchanged (care, not transformation), and the governance spend is justified by the asymmetry argument rather than the prevalence level. The number matters only to the safety-education posture's sizing, which is why appendix ask #4 queues it without gating anything. A strategy that needed that number before acting would wait years; one that pretends to know it would be invented. This deck does neither — the programme's evidence discipline, applied to its most sensitive gap.
