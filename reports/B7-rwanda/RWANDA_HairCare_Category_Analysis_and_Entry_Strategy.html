The consumer decode in one story

Rwanda's hair story is a compressed version of the continent's, with a national inflection. The grandmother's generation anointed hair and skin with amavuta y'inka — cow ghee, still sold in 500-gram tubs on Kasha today — and wore Amasunzu crests that told the world her family's standing. The colonial and post-colonial decades taught that natural hair was unprofessional; the relaxer and the hot comb did the teaching. The daughter's generation is running the correction: the big-chop wave documented in Kigali since 2022, the natural-hair salons, the heritage revival that puts Amasunzu back on stages and wedding photography. But the correction has a cost problem — natural hair, properly kept, takes more salon-hours and more product knowledge than relaxed hair, and the artisan brands serving the movement are priced for Kiyovu, not for the nation. An oil that makes home care between salon visits affordable, wearing a story the grandmother would recognise, priced where the movement's majority actually lives — that is not a marketing construct; it is the product the correction is waiting for. The platform line says exactly this and nothing more: your hair already knows oil. The brand's discipline is to serve the movement without claiming it — the heritage belongs to Rwanda, the bottle merely respects it.

Reading Hemani properly — the competitor as category author

Hemani deserves a fuller reading than a teardown row, because the entire verdict rests on reading it correctly. Hemani Herbals is a Karachi house with a global halal-wellness footprint; its Rwanda operation — an own-brand storefront plus marketplace stores — is a pattern it runs in dozens of secondary markets: plant a flag, price high, serve the willing, invest nothing in mass media or trade depth. That pattern is category AUTHORSHIP without category OWNERSHIP: Hemani taught Kigali that herbal amla oil is worth 10,000 francs, but built none of the machinery — radio familiarity, duka distribution, trade credit, local registration depth — that converts a price point into a franchise. The distinction matters because it predicts Hemani's response to Vatika: a house that hasn't invested in the market's machinery rarely starts investing under attack; the cheaper defence is a price cut on the storefront, which narrows the umbrella without closing the claim (Vatika's case was never price alone). The scenario that would genuinely hurt — Hemani signing a strong local distributor first — is exactly why the 90-day plan puts the distributor triage in weeks 1–4 and why the July window matters. In the race for Rwanda's herbal-hair franchise, Dabur's true competitor is not Hemani's brand; it is Hemani's potential distributor.

Teardown 5 — Dabur's own absence (hair-specific)

Sawa Citi's Isokko store carries Dabur toothpaste and Herbolene — but NO Vatika, no amla oil, no hair SKU of any kind [!] site-search confirm across Kasha/Goodlife/Murukali. The grey route chose oral and jelly, not hair — probably because Hemani's premium tier looked occupied to a wholesaler's eye. That is the claim's opening: the grey trade misread an umbrella as a wall.

SYNTHESIS: the shelf divides into a proof (Hemani, premium, distribution-poor), a mass tier that doesn't sell oils (Movit), a style gate that doesn't sell care (Darling), and artisans the market doesn't yet trust. Nobody holds brand + herbal story + distribution + fair price simultaneously. Vatika can hold all four in one launch wave — the definition of a CLAIM verdict.

DEEP THINK — why did nobody claim this already? The inversion that stress-tests the verdict: if the space under Hemani's umbrella is so obviously valuable, why is it empty? Candidate explanations. (1) The market is too small to notice — most multinationals run East Africa from Nairobi and sort countries by TAM; Rwanda's ~$240M FMCG pool doesn't clear their attention threshold. True, and irrelevant to Dabur, whose Tanzania infrastructure makes Rwanda's marginal cost a rounding error. (2) The sachet ban deterred the LUP players — the entrants who would normally storm this tier (Movit-class, Kenyan value brands) run sachet-first playbooks that are illegal here; the ban functions as an accidental moat protecting the bottle-native entrant. (3) Hemani's tier looked occupied from a distance — a wholesaler scanning shelf photos sees "herbal oils: taken" and moves on; only a category reading distinguishes an umbrella (price ceiling with nothing beneath) from a wall (defended tier). The grey trade made exactly this error, importing Dabur toothpaste but not Vatika. All three explanations favour entry; none suggests hidden rot. Second-order: the entrant most likely to follow Vatika in is Hemani itself, DOWNWARD — a mid-price Hemani line launched to defend. That fight is winnable on brand depth and distribution, but it argues for speed: the claim's value decays with every quarter the umbrella stands unchallenged. Kill-switch: if the July window is missed and Hemani or Movit moves first, re-derive rather than force — a contested claim is a different, worse deck.

The trial-rung problem, solved in the open

Because it is the deck's hardest design question, the no-sachet trial economics deserve their own passage. In Tanzania the sachet does three jobs at once: it prices trial at pocket money, it distributes through duka economics (a strip hangs on a nail), and it teaches the brand name through repetition. Rwanda's law removes the format, so the three jobs are reassigned. Trial pricing moves to the 90–100ml bottle at RWF 1,800–2,500 — more absolute money than a sachet, but Rwanda's wallet (27.4% national poverty vs Tanzania's 71% under $3.65) can reach it, and the bottle is a KEEPABLE object in a way a sachet never is: it sits on a shelf, re-seals, and advertises itself for weeks. Duka distribution moves to a six-bottle counter tray designed to occupy the same nail-and-counter geography the sachet strip would have. And name-teaching moves to the radio and the cashback mechanic — 500 francs back on a first purchase via MoMo does the "cheap first taste" work the sachet's price point would have done, targetable, measurable, and legal. The net design is arguably STRONGER than the sachet playbook it replaces: higher revenue per trial unit, a longer-lived brand object in the home, and a promo mechanic that builds a first-party customer list — the sachet never told you who bought it. This is the pattern the macro predicted: Rwanda's constraints, taken seriously, upgrade the playbook.

DEEP STRAT — the Kasha anchor deal, engineered. Kasha is the one channel in the batch that reaches the feature-phone rural woman directly — USSD ordering, mobile-money payment, discreet delivery, 600k+ customers, 61% female — and it has no anchor personal-care brand. The design: (i) Vatika as the platform's flagship herbal-hair line with category-anchor terms — preferential placement and a joint launch campaign in exchange for platform-exclusive pricing on one SKU (the R1 coconut bottle, making trial the platform's own offer); (ii) the reseller layer — Kasha serves resellers, and a Vatika reseller pack (6 × R1 at trade price) turns rural women into micro-distributors, echoing the stylist-as-distributor logic at feature-phone scale; (iii) the data covenant — monthly SKU-level sell-through by district, the market's only rural demand telemetry, contractually shared; (iv) the MoMo bridge — Kasha's payment rail and the cashback mechanic are the same infrastructure, so the promo runs natively on-platform first. Works if: the platform's economics can carry FMCG price points (their history is health/hygiene — adjacent, proven). Fails if: delivery costs swamp small-basket oil orders — then the reseller pack IS the model (aggregate baskets), and the direct-to-consumer layer waits for density. Cost: modest exclusivity concessions on one SKU. Value: a channel no competitor can copy without two years and a Series B.

Channel doctrine in prose: Rwanda's hair distribution is a two-hop system — Kigali holds the depth (modern trade, salons, marketplaces, Goodlife) and Kimironko's wholesale benches fan the rest of the country out through dukas the way Kariakoo serves Tanzania, at one-tenth the scale. The practical consequence: ONE Kigali depot with a disciplined wholesale price list covers the nation; there is no multi-city depot build, no regional distributor patchwork, no second warehouse until the border towns justify it at W2. That is the smallness dividend again — and it is why the distributor conversation (Sulfo or independent) is about fleet discipline and trade-terms enforcement, not geographic coverage. The one channel that breaks the two-hop pattern is Kasha, which ships from its own fulfilment directly to the customer — the reason it deserves anchor-partner engineering rather than a wholesale price row.

The salon system, resized honestly

Kigali's braid economy is real — knotless installs at RWF 30,000 in Kiyovu, a dedicated braiding section at Kimironko, Darling running a destination salon — but it is a tenth of Dar es Salaam's, and the wedge is sized accordingly: 150 stylists, Kigali-only at launch, recruited through the braid-supply wholesale benches at Kimironko rather than through a national canvass. The economics are the Tanzania design at Rwandan prices (chair-side R4 bulk packs, stylist margin on retail bottles, the 10-minute under-braid ritual), with one Rwanda-specific addition: because the stylist herself is likely a MoMo merchant, her chair-side sales ride the same cashback mechanic as retail — the programme's promotional and channel infrastructure are one system here, which no other Batch-7 market's cash-heavy salon economy allowed. If the 150 convert well, Musanze and Huye add 50 each at W2; if they don't, the programme degrades to sampling as designed in Tanzania. The wedge is a lever, not a pillar — Rwanda's pillar is the duka tray plus the radio.

Regulatory sequencing — the quarter that decides the year

The regulatory table compresses a sequencing argument worth spelling out. Rwanda's per-SKU registration economics (US$1,250 each, no bundling) reward a narrow launch — the amla-coconut pair, plus the oral and jelly SKUs the formalisation wave carries, is a ~US$8–10k registration bill; a thirty-SKU portfolio landing would cost more in fees than the first year's contribution. The import-visa rule means the paper must clear before the container sails from Dar — so the W0 filing window (January–April) is not administrative padding but the critical path, and it runs concurrently with the Kwibuka-dark quarter when no marketing could run anyway. The calendar and the bureaucracy, read together, hand the programme a naturally correct sequence: file through the mourning season, land in the liberation season, advertise into Christmas. A plan that fights this rhythm pays twice; the plan that rides it pays nothing extra at all.
