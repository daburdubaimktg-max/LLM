C1 · The finding: SURVEYED-NOT-SHELVED

Burundi's oral category presents an inversion the queue has not seen: the CLINICAL record exists and the COMMERCIAL record does not. The first-ever national oral-health survey of Burundi (Medicina 2023 — WHO methodology, 1,007 six-year-olds + 895 twelve-year-olds; dmft/DMFT varying significantly by age, living area and region; being twelve and living in southern provinces protective at OR 0.52 and 0.26) means a Dabur deck can cite PEER-REVIEWED caries data for this market — a rarity anywhere in the frontier queue (ID — mdpi.com/PMC10535420, 2023). Yet not one toothpaste shelf price, market-share figure, per-capita-consumption number or distributor name survived the 403 wall. Colgate is near-certainly present (it is ubiquitous across the EAC, served via Nairobi/Tanzanian re-export) but its price is a gap; no local dentifrice manufacture exists (Savonor is soap/oils); the miswak practice among the ~2.5–5% Muslim minority is plausible but uncaptured and too small to lead.

The fifty-sixth corridor finding, stated for the register: where a category is documented in the CLINIC (a peer-reviewed disease dataset) but blank on the SHELF (no captured commercial data), size the OPPORTUNITY from the epidemiology and design the ENTRY from the register the tariff wall does not tax — herbal-trust differentiation, not commodity price. The survey sizes the need; the wall dictates the axis. That two-clause rule is the finding's whole portable content, and it travels to any market where a clinical record outruns the commercial one: let the epidemiology decide whether to enter, let the tariff decide how, and never let either pretend to be the other. This finding fuses the queue's two Burundi truths: the disease burden is real and citable (so the category matters), but the 35% CET wall (WALLED-OUT-OF-PRICE, the hair deck's finding) means Dabur cannot win oral on commodity price against Colgate's established distribution any more than it could win hair against Movit. The escape is the same: the herbal register. A Meswak/Dabur Herb'l paste does not compete on "cheaper than Colgate" (the wall forbids it and the fluoride law forbids the attack copy anyway); it competes on the herbal-trust culture the state itself legitimises (the traditional-medicine decree; ≥1,000 tradipraticiens) — a register Colgate does not occupy and the tariff cannot erase. And the fluoride law governs the whole thing: the survey's caries crisis is an argument FOR brushing and fluoride, never against them, so the herbal paste stands BESIDE modern dentistry, carrying fluoride itself, never posturing as its natural alternative.

C7c · The India-credibility asset — and its anchor to work against

Burundi hands the herbal register one genuine tailwind and one genuine trap, and the deck must hold both. The tailwind: CAMEBU's public-tender medicine supply is largely Indian-origin (ID), so "India = medicines" is a real, earned association in the health system — an Indian herbal-plus-fluoride paste inherits a credibility a random import would not. The trap: the same association anchors "Indian = cheap generic", and a herbal paste sold on the CARE register at a small premium must lift ABOVE that anchor rather than ride it down into the price fight the wall already lost. The resolution is register discipline: the paste's Indian origin is a quiet credibility fact (present in the trade conversation, where the pharmacist knows the CAMEBU supply chain) but never a price claim (absent from consumer copy, where "Indian generic" would drag it toward Colgate's cheap-tube axis). The herbal-trust register — plants, tradition, the state's own legitimising decree — is what lifts the paste above the generic anchor, because it competes on a quality the generic association does not touch. India buys the paste its way in the door; the herbal register is what keeps it from being filed on the bottom shelf once inside. The line the deck walks is narrow and worth stating twice: the CAMEBU credibility is a trade-conversation asset, spoken to the pharmacist who knows the supply chain; it is never a consumer claim, because the moment "Indian" reaches the shopper as a price signal rather than a quality one, the wall's lost price fight reopens on the worst possible terms. This is the "origin stance" the macro flagged, executed at the category level: use the medical credibility, refuse the generic anchor, and let the herbal register carry the premium the wall requires.

C9b · The bread test, re-derived at the parallel rate

The demand model borrows Madagascar's bread test and re-derives it for Burundi's FX regime. At the épicerie/boutique till, a household at the US$478 wallet spends its coins on staples first, and the mini-tube's ~2,600 BIF asks for a small, occasional discretionary coin — but here the coin's value is itself unstable, because everything imported is priced off the swinging parallel rate. So the Burundi bread test has a second axis Madagascar's did not: not only "in which weeks does the till allow a toothpaste purchase" but "at this week's parallel rate, is the herbal premium still a small coin or has FX widened it into a visible one." The mini-tube's job is to keep the premium small in absolute BIF even as the rate moves — which is why the trial format leads and the wholesale terms hold stable across quarters (the entry absorbs FX noise rather than passing it weekly to the boutique shelf, the same discipline the hair deck set). The care register's premium survives only if it stays a small coin; the moment the parallel rate turns it into a large one, the mini-tube's absolute price is the shock absorber. Format follows the competitor AND the currency here — the mini fights both non-consumption and FX volatility, and a tube on the shelf at a stable small premium is the answer to both.

DEEP THINK · Three objections answered

Objection 1 — "You have a peer-reviewed national caries dataset and you refuse to use it in advertising. That is wasting your best evidence." Answer: the survey is the best evidence for the BOARDROOM and the worst evidence for the shelf. It sizes the need and maps its geography — exactly what a country team needs to decide whether and where to enter — but a caries statistic in a consumer ad is fear copy, and fear copy sells nothing a mother doesn't already feel every time her child winces. The fluoride law compounds the point: the survey's crisis is an argument for brushing and fluoride, so any Dabur use of it that implied "our herbal paste fixes what the statistics show" would be both fear-mongering and a fluoride-law breach. The discipline is to let the survey do its real job — need-sizing and territory targeting — and to sell the paste on the care register the shopper actually responds to. Using the best boardroom evidence as shelf copy would waste it twice: once by cheapening a serious dataset, once by breaking the law that governs the category.

Objection 2 — "Herbal differentiation at a premium, in the poorest market in the queue, behind a 35% wall — is there any real business here?"
