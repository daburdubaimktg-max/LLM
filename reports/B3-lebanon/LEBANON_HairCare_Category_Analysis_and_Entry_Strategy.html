C1 — The ask (executive summary)

Macro anchor (LEBANON_MACRO decision board): Hair is ranked LEAD — the one category where Dabur is already listed, correctly localised, boycott-neutral and priced into penetration, in a market actively rewarding natural claims and hard-water efficacy. The job is not entry; it is fixing three self-inflicted ceilings: the grocery-only cage, the value-floor trap, and the under-marketed hammam-zeit heritage.

The play in one screen. Four truths define the lead. First, the beachhead is real and under-exploited: Vatika and Amla sit in Spinneys and Carrefour at the branded-oil value floor ($2.09–4.49), with a correctly-named Vatika Hammam Zaith already on shelf — Dabur is the only player whose SKU carries the actual ritual word — yet the brand is absent from the pharmacy-beauty channel that matters disproportionately in Lebanon and absent from the premium tier that carries the margin. Second, the market's own growth axis points at Dabur: Euromonitor names pollution and mineral-heavy water as the local product-choice driver, and no incumbent owns the hard-water/clarifying/anti-buildup territory sharply — a functional problem every Lebanese head experiences, cleaner and more ownable than an amla-heritage story that reads foreign. Third, the channel arbitrage is already proven: Beauty Pharm sells the identical Amla 100ml at $4.00 that Spinneys sells at $2.09 — a +91% pharmacy premium that exists today without any Dabur effort — so the tier-up is not a hypothesis, it is an observed price architecture waiting to be managed. Fourth, the boycott tailwind is real but belongs to someone else at the top: the Lebanese-pride lane (Beesline, Khan Al-Saboun) is not Dabur's to take; Dabur's win is the "herbal oil that isn't boycotted and isn't cheap" position, taking share from the BDS-exposed mass-herbal tier (Garnier Ultra Doux, Elvive oil-replacement, Sunsilk argan). The move: (1) break the grocery cage — pharmacy + Feel22 + Mazen listings via a health-channel distributor; (2) tier-up — launch a $5–8 حمّام زيت / anti-hard-water treatment line, argan-fronted, amla-as-active; (3) own hard water — a clarifying/anti-mineral-buildup hero; (4) borrow salon credibility via stylist reels and "salon hot-oil ritual at home"; (5) trilingual creative in Lebanese dialect + French, re-owning the fading heritage word. Do not fight Beesline for the local-pride lane, do not lead amla as heritage (it's the active, not the banner), do not campaign on the boycott, and do not stay in the $2–4 floor (the "cheap Indian oil" trap). Reputation Bet (C18): by Q4 2027, pharmacy + beauty-e-tail listings secured and a $4.50–7 treatment tier launched, taking share from Garnier/Elvive — or Vatika stays a grocery-floor oil and the LEAD reverts to a passive listing.

C4 — Demand engine: the hammam zeit ritual & the hard-water hook (Module — consumer truth)

The demand engine has two layers — a ritual and a problem — and Dabur is uniquely placed on both. The ritual: حمّام زيت (hammam zeit, the "oil bath") — warm oil massaged into scalp and lengths, capped and left for an hour or overnight, then washed — is a living Levantine practice, both salon and home, using argan, olive, coconut, jojoba, almond and (in Dabur's world) amla. The problem: Beirut's mildly-to-moderately hard, mineral-heavy water plus urban pollution — dull, limp, brittle hair, itchy flaky scalp, accelerated breakage — which Euromonitor itself names as the category's product-choice driver.

So what: Dabur does not need to invent demand — the ritual exists (and Dabur alone names it), the problem is officially the category's growth axis, and the salon culture supplies credibility on loan. The Jordan lesson transfers with a twist: in Amman the move was "scale the branded hammam zait"; in Beirut it is "re-own the fading word and attach it to the modern problem" — the hot-oil ritual repositioned as the anti-hard-water treatment.

The amla-vs-argan resolution — front the familiar, carry the active. The honest tension in Dabur's Lebanon hair story is that its global hero ingredient, amla (أملا, Indian gooseberry), is locally under-known — it reads Indian, not Levantine — while the oils Lebanese women trust are argan (اركان, Moroccan but pan-Levant) and olive (Lebanese patrimony, owned by Khan Al-Saboun). The resolution is architectural, not apologetic: lead the shelf and the creative with argan and the hammam-zeit ritual — words that need no education — and position amla as the functional "secret active" for hair-fall and thickness, exactly the way Western brands deploy "keratin" or "hyaluron" as science words no consumer grew up with. Amla does not need to beat olive on heritage; it needs to earn a job (تساقط الشعر hair-fall control) that argan and olive don't explicitly claim. Dabur's range already supports this architecture: Vatika Argan shampoo/conditioner up front, Amla Hair Fall and Anti-Dandruff behind, Hammam Zaith as the ritual crown. Halal stays a quiet badge (mixed religion — macro rule), and the French register ("huile capillaire," "soin profond") runs alongside Arabic for the Francophone third.

C8 — Territories / where to win first

Lead in Beirut + Mount Lebanon (macro security rule; retail + pharmacy + salon + creator density), then the coastal north, deferring the south. The territorial logic follows the money and the channels, not the population map: the dollar-have shopper who funds the treatment tier is concentrated in Beirut and Mount Lebanon, where Spinneys, the pharmacy-beauty retailers and the celebrity salons all cluster within a short drive of each other — meaning one wave-one push covers the modern-trade doors, the pharmacy break-in and the stylist-collab shoots simultaneously. Tripoli and the north are a value play (small packs, dukkan, the $2.09 floor), not a treatment play. The south is deferred on the macro security rule, and — a second-order point — it is also Khan Al-Saboun's Sidon heartland, where an outsider natural brand is least welcome; when Dabur does extend there, it goes as value grocery, never with pride-lane creative.
