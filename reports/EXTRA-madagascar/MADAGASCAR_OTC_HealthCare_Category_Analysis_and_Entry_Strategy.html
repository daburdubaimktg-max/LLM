C1 · The finding: GARDEN-KEPT

Every prior OTC file in this queue asked some version of "how does a herbal house enter a market that lacks herbal infrastructure?" Madagascar inverts the question with a completeness no other market has approached. This is a country whose flagship research institute — IMRA (Institut Malgache de Recherches Appliquées, founded by the Pasteur-trained Prof. Albert Rakoto Ratsimamanga) — has developed ~40 plant-derived medicines including Madaglucyl, the first plant-based antidiabetic, with five international patents (CAPTURED — imra-ratsimamanga.com). Its traditional practitioners are state-recognised in four legal categories since décret n°2007-805, reporting monthly to the Ministry of Health (CAPTURED — Cairn/Autrepart 2017). Its natural-wellness retail is locally owned at chain scale: Homeopharma's ~40 own institutes island-wide selling essential oils, plant soaps and wellness lines at modest prices (CAPTURED — madacamp). Its national remedy — ravintsara (Cinnamomum camphora CT 1,8-cineole, "the leaf good for everything", in the pharmacopoeia since at least 1957) — is simultaneously a household steam-and-infusion practice and the flagship of a world-scale essential-oil export economy: ~50% of world clove-oil supply, ~25% of ylang-ylang, 95% of production exported to ~40 countries (CAPTURED — Pranarôm; Madagascar Matin; ekl-export).

The fifty-second corridor finding, stated for the register: where the market has already industrialised its own pharmacopoeia, the herbal entrant's cultural fit is NOT an entry lane — it is a seating chart. Dabur cannot sell "plants that heal" to the country that taught its neighbours the concept; it can only sell FORMATS AND ADJACENCIES the kept garden does not grow. The Seychelles' REMEDY-FORMATTED logic (#195) scales here to institutional size: the belief system is intact, the supply system is world-class, and the gap is specific — Madagascar's herbal economy is built on OILS, EXTRACTS and RAW EXPORT, not on FMCG convenience formats. Nobody local mass-produces a menthol-camphor balm tin, a honey-ginger lozenge, or a shelf-stable herbal syrup at épicerie prices; those formats are imported, thinly, or absent. The entry therefore walks a narrow, honest lane: two to three convenience formats, papered through the AMM's parapharmacy scope, distributed via Madaphar's pharmacy network and the épicerie tier, with every asset speaking AS A GUEST — explicit respect for fanafody gasy (Malagasy traditional medicine), zero claims of superiority, and the national remedy (ravintsara) never competed with, imitated, or name-dropped for warmth.

C3b · Why the strongest fit yields the narrowest entry — the finding's arithmetic

The finding's central paradox deserves its arithmetic spelled out, because it reverses the intuition a board brings to "great cultural fit". In a market with WEAK herbal infrastructure, Dabur's fit is a lane: the belief must be built, and whoever builds it owns the category (the programme has played that hand across a dozen markets). In a market with STRONG infrastructure, the same fit is a constraint, because every use of it is already occupied by a keeper with more right to it — the science by IMRA, the practice by the tradipraticiens, the retail by Homeopharma, the raw value by the export engine, the sacred centre by ravintsara. Even the export engine's raw value — the clove oil, the ylang, the geranium hydrolats — is spoken for by distilleries and cooperatives with decades of world trade behind them. What remains unoccupied is precisely the part of the herbal economy that requires NO cultural authority to serve: the convenience format. A tin that warms a chest and a strip that soothes a throat make no claim about Malagasy botanical wisdom; they make a claim about packaging and price, which is the one register a guest may speak freely. So the entry's width is inversely proportional to the garden's completeness — and Madagascar's garden is the most complete in the queue. The arithmetic's payoff is in the margins: because the entry competes on format convenience rather than on belief or commodity, it earns the set's healthiest contribution margins (~21%/~18%) from its narrowest range. Narrow-and-profitable is not a compromise here; it is the finding's direct consequence, and any attempt to widen the range would move Dabur out of the empty bed and into a keeper's, trading margin and welcome for volume the garden will resent.

C7b · The pharmacist as the launch's keystone relationship

The set's practice of naming the most important non-customer lands, in this deck, on the pharmacist. In a 200-pharmacy country whose street-medicine tier is proliferating and whose AMM is publicly alarmed, the licensed pharmacist is simultaneously the papered lane's gatekeeper, the category's most trusted voice, and the enemy of exactly the informal trade Dabur refuses to touch — which makes the pharmacist the entry's natural ally rather than a gate to be worked. The relationship is built in sequence, the Behram's-warming pattern from the Seychelles re-derived: first the balm and lozenge arrive papered and batch-coded through Madaphar (the pharmacist sees compliance before he sees a sales pitch); then the evidence card — ingredients, classification, the honest comfort-claim — sits at the counter as a fact sheet, not a promotion; then, only after the first season's reorders prove the formats move, the conversation about the syrup ascent opens. The pharmacist's trust, once earned, is worth more than any media spend the 403-walled market could absorb: his recommendation IS the launch's advertising, and it is free, durable and impossible to buy directly. The deck's discipline is therefore to earn it slowly and never to spend it — a recommended brand that starts pushing promotions at the counter converts a keystone relationship into a transaction, and the pharmacist, who has the whole shelf to recommend from, simply recommends something else. The same logic sets the lane's whole tempo: earn slowly, spend never, and let compliance and consistency do the work a media budget would do in a market the 403 wall did not seal — the pharmacist's word is the advertising, and it is bought only with conduct.

DEEP THINK · Three objections answered

Objection 1 — "If the cultural fit is the strongest in the programme, entering with two humble formats wastes the fit." Answer: the fit's value is not a licence to expand; it is the reason the narrow entry will be WELCOMED rather than resisted. A market that believes in plants does not need Dabur to teach it belief — it needs tins and strips its own industry doesn't make, and it will judge the guest by whether he knows which beds are his. The fit pays out over years, not at launch: it makes the pharmacist's evidence card easy, the trade conversations warm, the eventual syrup ascent natural, and the IMRA conversation possible. Spending the fit at launch on a wide claim-heavy range would convert the programme's best welcome into its fastest wear-out. The garden rewards guests who stay guests longest.

Objection 2 — "Refusing a honey jar in a honey-rich market ignores an obvious volume line." Answer: the refusal IS the strategy's proof of comprehension. An imported jar in Madagascar competes with litchi honey that blushes rose, mokarana honey that exists nowhere else on earth, and a cooperative economy that lifts beekeepers over the poverty line at twenty hives — a fight that loses commercially (the import is worse-storied at a higher landed cost) and reputationally (the entrant read as not understanding the country's own treasure). The lozenge takes honey's UNIVERSAL belief into a format the terroir does not occupy; the sourcing watch keeps the door open to someday buying the terroir's story on the terroir's terms. That is the whole quartet's teaching compressed: honey is a language before it is a product, and verdict #36 speaks it correctly here.

Objection 3 — "The two-lane paper split (pharmacy vs épicerie) doubles complexity for a two-SKU entry." Answer: the split is inherited, not built — Madaphar and the Tsaralalàna house were both signed by the macro's two-door structure, and the paper distinction (what carries health claims goes papered; what is grocery-legal comfort goes grocery) is the AMM's own architecture, not Dabur's invention. Collapsing the lanes to save complexity is how herbal entrants end up explaining themselves to regulators: a lozenge with throat words in an épicerie till-tray is a small daily illegality that compounds. The split costs one extra terms annex and buys the papered walk's whole reputational dividend — in a market whose street-medicine tier is proliferating and whose AMM is publicly alarmed, being VISIBLY on paper is the cheapest brand asset the deck owns.
