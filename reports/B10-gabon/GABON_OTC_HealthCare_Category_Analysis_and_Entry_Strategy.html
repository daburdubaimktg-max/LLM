C1 — Category verdict & thesis

Verdict: BUILD — "the lane Congo inferred, Gabon wrote into law." Congo-Brazzaville's OTC deck built a food lane out of a regulatory silence and gated the whole entry on a counsel opinion. Gabon hands the same strategy a statute: ANMAPS's own schedule names a «complément alimentaire» category with a flat 50,000-FCFA fee and a light administrative dossier, a «médicament traditionnel amélioré» (MTA) lane purpose-built for phyto-remedies, and a cosmetics/hygiene lane for Lal Tail — while AGASA covers plain food and the DGCCRF's 24-hour AMC clears customs paperwork. The entry architecture therefore inverts Congo's: not one gate and a workaround, but four named doors, each cheap, and none of them the full-CTD medicine lane (which the 2026 pharma-VAT change and the LSP generics factory make unattractive anyway — Dabur does not fight Doliprane at 1,125 FCFA or an Indian-founded local plant selling 30–40% under market). Demand-side, the CNAMGS crisis is the engine: the insurer that covers ~40% of the population stopped being honoured by pharmacies for a week in January 2025 over 7–8bn FCFA of unpaid debts, its co-payment reform collapsed in September 2025, and medicines still eat ~70% of household health spending [!] — cash self-care is not a segment here, it is the system. The portfolio answer has a programme first in it: Chyawanprash gets its first real slot. In Congo and Libya the jar tonic was deferred for lack of schema; in Gabon the schema EXISTS and is expensive — Sainte-Marie's live shelf sells Arkopharma phyto-capsules at 11,000–16,215, Berocca at 22,700, Bion 3 at 22,450, across a 105-SKU supplements catalogue — so the Ayurvedic tonic enters as the value-premium challenger to French phyto, with IPHAMETRA (the state's own traditional-pharmacopoeia institute, whose MTA mission literally shares a name with the registration lane) as the legitimacy partner, and the new Health Code — which just made traditional medicine a co-equal third pillar of the health system — as the policy wind at its back (strategist read on agent evidence, 8 Jul 2026).

C2 — Market size & growth

Sizing honestly: medicines run ~25% of Gabon's total health expenditure (~60bn FCFA/year [!] PNDS-sourced, re-validate) in a top-5-in-Africa per-capita health-spend country — but the structure matters more than the total. The insured 40% face an insurer in rolling crisis (the January 2025 dispensing suspension; the abandoned 50% co-pay; WHO-adjacent credibility headlines), the poor 34.6% face pharmacy prices built on an import stack the 2026 Finance Law just made heavier, and both cohorts resolve the squeeze the same way: cash, at the counter, on their own judgment — or on the street's (pharmacies de rue at Nkembo and Nzeng-Ayong selling antibiotics by the tablet; share unquantified, the honest gap this deck refuses to fill with Congo's number). The visible formal market splits into a French-brand pharmacy shelf (Doliprane's 1,125–1,540 analgesic floor; a 105-SKU supplements catalogue running 6,258–25,000) and a modern-trade food shelf where imported honey already dominates urban consumption (Europe/Brazil/Cameroon — FAO-confirmed) at 7,800–15,600 FCFA/kg retail against a 2,000-FCFA village gate. Growth logic for Dabur: undercut French phyto with equal-dignity Ayurveda (the supplements shelf's prices are a colonial inheritance, not a cost floor), win the import-honey shelf on certification and constancy (no local-pride wall exists here — the anti-Congo), and ride the recovery/vitality demand a 56-per-1,000 malaria incidence generates without ever touching therapeutics (strategist read).

C3 — Consumer & ritual decode

Three registers organise Gabonese self-care. The first is counter trust: public tertiary care carries a devastating reputation («on y entre malade, on peut en sortir mort» — the Gabonreview verdict on the CHUL), the insurer's word is publicly doubted, and the pharmacist — organised, politically potent (Sypharga negotiated directly with the president), professionally regulated — is the health system's last trusted interface. Dabur's comms architecture follows: the pharmacist is detailed and equipped first, the radio explains, the POS proves. The second register is the newly dignified traditional pillar: Gabon's first Health Code (15 April [!] year 2025/26) made traditional medicine a co-equal third component of the national system, legally recognising the nganga, herboriste and tradithérapeute — and IPHAMETRA runs open days telling the public traditional medicine «ne doit pas être perçue comme une pratique honteuse». Ayurveda enters this conversation as a SISTER pharmacopoeia — scientific, codified, institution-backed — through IPHAMETRA's door, never through the nganga's: the spiritual register (Bwiti, iboga, nkisi-class vocabulary) remains the programme's untouchable ceiling, and the deck's rule is partner the institute, never the initiation. The third register is discovery-versus-purchase schizophrenia: Gabonese consumers discover health products on TikTok-class short video (the ban report names TikTok as the live marketplace) but the legal purchase happens at a licensed counter — so content teaches, pharmacies transact, and Dabur never runs a checkout on social (register read; Myènè lexicon queued — the Libreville launch market's own language).

C4 — Competitor teardown (Module 1)

The teardown has four blocs and two absences. Bloc one: French pharmacy brands — Sanofi's Doliprane owns the analgesic floor at honest prices (1,125–1,540); Arkopharma, Bayer (Berocca) and Bion 3 own the supplements shelf at prices that read like import-stack arithmetic plus colonial habit (11,000–22,700). They are the price umbrella, not the volume enemy. Bloc two: LSP — the Indian-founded Nkok factory, government-championed, selling ~30 generics 30–40% under market; currently at 1% capacity awaiting a state rescue with 30% state ownership mooted: strategically it is (a) the reason Dabur refuses commodity analgesics, and (b) the proof that Indian pharmaceutical manufacturing is a POLICY-FAVOURED model here — a long-horizon JV signal the corporate memo should note. Bloc three: the wholesale duopoly — UbiPharm Gabon and Laborex Gabon (ex-Pharmagabon, Eurapharma/CFAO network, since 1996): mature, foreign-networked, both natural first calls, with the caution that their historical entanglement needs a competitive-map check [!]. Bloc four: the street tier — real, located (Nkembo, Nzeng-Ayong), unit-dose-priced, unquantified. The absences: Shalina is NOT in Gabon (its own market list skips the country — the region's affordable-OTC shadow incumbent simply isn't here), and no Indian OTC brand is visible at retail — the shelf Dabur wants is genuinely unclaimed (structural read on agent evidence).
