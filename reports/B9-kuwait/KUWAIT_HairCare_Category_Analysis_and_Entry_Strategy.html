DEFEND-AND-PREMIUMISE DECK — Dabur's own investor disclosures state Vatika Hair Cream is the #1 volume share in Kuwait and Vatika Hammam Zaith the #1 hot-oil treatment [!] re-validate FY + share source. This is not an entry problem. It is a leadership-defence problem with three named threats: a deep-promo price signal Dabur itself created, a P&G premium flank already scent-localised (H&S Oud at KWD 2.190), and a co-op national shelf where leadership has not been converted into presence.

C1 — The ask

The decision this deck asks for: approve the HAMMAM ZAITH programme — (1) repair the Vatika price signal (the 2×400ml Dandruff Guard at KWD 0.890 is selling the category leader at 44% of its single-pack per-ml price on the national-facing shelf); (2) convert hidden format leadership (hair cream, hot-oil) into visible co-op presence with premium-signalled heritage packs; (3) own the dust-season platform (Jun–Jul shamal, 42–46°C summers) that no brand has branded — before P&G extends the Oud lesson from scent to season. Budget shape: programme money from the 0%-duty/no-VAT corridor, zero new list-price cuts.

Brand-by-brand teardown — how the winners actually won (Module 1).

1. Head & Shoulders (P&G via AAW). Entered on the derm-clinical rung and never left it: «شامبو ضد القشرة رقم 1 في العالم» on the Arabia site, TikTok derm personas («القشرة ما تختفي بالصدفة… العلامة رقم ١ اللي يوصي بها أطباء الجلدية»), creator ads with colloquial hooks («القشرة؟ روحة بلا رجعة»). Then the masterstroke: Oud Collection at KWD 2.190 — taking a functional brand up-market via scent-status, +35% over core H&S, confirmed on LuLu Kuwait shelf. Lesson: in Kuwait you premiumise through culture, not through molecule counts. Won through AAW's co-op muscle — the same door Dabur has not opened.

2. Sunsilk (Unilever). The mid-market volume machine: 400+180ml banded at KWD 1.195, assorted-variant strategy, feminine everyday codes. Wins on ubiquity and banded value, not on claims. Its seat (≈1.2 KWD/400ml) is exactly the corridor Vatika should hold non-promo — today Vatika promos straight through it down to 0.890/2×400, vacating the mid seat it could own at full margin.

3. Parachute (Marico). Coconut-oil price anchor: 200ml at 0.395, 1L at 1.720. Wins the diaspora kitchen-shelf ritual on trust + per-ml economics. Dabur's counter is already correct — Vatika 125ml at 0.325 undercuts the entry pack while Amla differentiates on ingredient story — but the banded Amla-500ml+shampoo pack shows the real play: use oil trust to recruit into wash, where margins live.

4. Himalaya (via Safwan Trading). The herbal ladder-climber: Anti-Hair-Fall 400ml at 1.320 — priced above Sunsilk, near Elvive. Himalaya prices herbal at a premium in Kuwait (mirroring its 2.090 face-wash anchor) and holds full pharmacy distribution Dabur lacks. Lesson: Kuwaiti shoppers pay more for herbal when the register is clinical-natural, not ethnic-value. Vatika's promo depth actively contradicts this lesson today.

5. L'Oréal Elvive / TRESemmé. The masstige ceiling-setters: Elvive Extraordinary Oil 400ml ~1.250–1.700, TRESemmé Keratin 600ml at 2.860 — salon-code language ("keratin", "72h smooth") sold through hypers and Boots. They hold the aspirational shelf voice but have no heritage claim; their well-documented weakness is ritual authenticity, which is Dabur's deepest asset.

What the five teardowns say together. Every winner on this shelf won by choosing one register and funding it for years: H&S bought clinical authority and then converted it into cultural premium; Sunsilk bought ubiquity; Parachute bought per-ml trust; Himalaya bought the clinical-natural middle and — the detail that matters most — proved Kuwaitis pay a premium for herbal when the packaging refuses to look cheap; the masstige houses bought salon language. Nobody won by discounting: the only brand on the shelf currently signalling desperation-depth pricing is the category's own format leader. That is the strategic absurdity this deck exists to fix. The second cross-cutting lesson is distribution symmetry: every non-Indian winner rides a politically-wired national house (AAW for P&G, Safwan for Himalaya's pharmacy lane), while Dabur's presumed route runs through the diaspora circuit — leadership built on maybe 40% of the market's doors [!] audit. The third lesson is that culture beats molecule in Kuwait's premium tier: H&S did not premiumise with a new active, it premiumised with oud. Dabur owns more of that cultural raw material — hammam, henna, oil ritual — than every competitor combined, and monetises almost none of it at premium price points.

Demand synthesis. The grid exposes why "hair care Kuwait" is really three businesses. The Kuwaiti national business is a premium ritual-and-status market reached through co-ops, salons and Snapchat, where Dabur's equity is latent but unmerchandised. The settled-expat family business is a mid-market loyalty machine Dabur already leads through LuLu and the diaspora e-grocers. The deck's moves map one-to-one: W2 serves the first, W1 protects the second, and the diaspora ladder in T14 quarantines the third. Media weight follows the margin pool, not the volume pool — which is why Snapchat and the co-op leaflet, not Hindi-language YouTube, take the incremental dinar.

DEEP THINK — on W2, the premium heritage line. Inversion: to guarantee failure, launch premium Vatika while the 0.890 twin-pack sits on the same LuLu gondola — the shopper sees both, the committee sees both, and the premium story dies of self-contradiction. Sequencing is the strategy. Second-order: if premium Vatika works, P&G's rational counter is an H&S Oud line-extension blitz (conditioner, cream) through AAW's co-op relationships — so Dabur must lock its co-op listings and Snapchat creator roster in the same quarter it launches, denying the fast-follow window. Base rate: heritage-brand premiumisation in GCC hair has worked (H&S Oud, TRESemmé keratin) roughly when the premium cue is cultural (scent, ritual) and failed when it is merely "better formula" — Vatika's hammam/oud cue sits on the winning side. Kill-switch: if after two leaflet cycles the premium line's rate-of-sale in its 10 pilot co-ops is <40% of category-premium norms, stop the rollout, keep the line exclusive to Sultan Center + Boots as a halo, and reallocate to W3.

DEEP STRAT — why the dust season is a platform and not a promotion. Kuwait's weather calendar is the most violent in the GCC: a recorded 53.9°C maximum, 42–46°C summer norms, and a June–July shamal cycle that puts a visible layer of dust on every car, balcony and scalp in the country. The consumer behaviour already exists — wash frequency spikes, salons report post-storm bookings, pharmacies sell more scalp products — but no brand has named the occasion. Naming an occasion is the cheapest durable asset in FMCG marketing: "back-to-school" and "Ramadan pantry" were both invented by marketers, and their inventors still tax them. «موسم الغبار» is available, ownable, and renews itself every single year without media spend once established. The platform also solves Vatika's register problem elegantly: it is functional without being medical (no MOH exposure), seasonal without being promotional (kits, not discounts), and it fuses the oil ritual (pre-storm protection) with wash (post-storm cleanse) — moving the whole portfolio, not one SKU. The claim discipline is the only hard constraint: protect-and-cleanse cosmetic language, never treatment verbs, and the Snapchat creative should lean on the visual comedy of dust-day life that Kuwaiti creators already produce organically every June.

Reading T14 as a doctrine, not a price list. The two ladders are not two price tiers — they are two complete commercial systems that must never appear on the same shelf. The national-facing ladder trades margin for signal: fewer SKUs, no BOGO, gift-grade packaging, and the multi-Gulf registration marks that tell a co-op committee this is a serious regional house. The diaspora ladder trades signal for velocity: banded packs, leaflet features, and the per-ml value that a KD-800-threshold bachelor remitter rationally demands. The boundary between them is enforced by assortment, not hope — the premium line simply has no barcode presence in the diaspora circuit, and sub-1.000 twin-packs have none in co-ops or on the LuLu aisle a committee member walks. Where the two shoppers do meet (henna, entry oils), the pack is identical and honestly priced in both systems, because those are trust formats where a visible price gap between channels would damage both ladders at once.

The channel arithmetic behind the priorities. If A4 holds even loosely — co-ops at roughly half of category value — then Dabur's current system, however dominant inside LuLu and the diaspora e-grocers, is competing for well under half the market. The margin table sharpens the point: the co-op node is not even expensive relative to hypers once listing fees are annualised; what it costs is politics — committee relationships, Kuwaiti-fronted representation, patience across leaflet cycles. That is precisely the spend the 0%-duty/no-VAT corridor funds without touching price. Pharmacy, by contrast, is a signal investment: at 5–8% of category value it will never drive the P&L, but Boots' 21 doors are where the premium line earns the right to exist and where Himalaya demonstrates weekly that herbal can hold a 1.320 price point. Q-comm is the watch-channel: tMart's sub-30-minute default and Kanbkam's price-transparency layer mean every promo decision is now visible to every shopper instantly — one more reason deep-promo signalling can no longer be contained to one channel.
