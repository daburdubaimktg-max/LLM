UGANDA — HAIR CARE

Category Analysis & Entry Strategy for Dabur India · Codename: AMAFUTA (Luganda: "the oils")

Batch 7 (SSA East) · Prepared 5 July 2026 · Public sources only · Companion to the Uganda Macro Foundation · Luganda gap rule applied

BEACHHEAD DECK — hair is the macro's BET category and the whole Uganda programme's spearhead. The market is US$0.48bn and growing 6.6% [!] Statista outlook, its median consumer is a teenager, and Dabur's oils are already on the shelf — Vatika and Amla flow in through fragmented importers and hold a dedicated Jumia Uganda brand page. The strategy deliberately splits the category: fight in oils and moisture (Indian oiling tradition meets Afro-hair moisture need, no entrenched owner), and refuse the fights Dabur cannot win — relaxers belong to Movit's Radiant and TCB, braids and extensions to Godrej's Darling factory in Namanve. One category, three markets; Dabur enters the one that matches its equity.

C1 — The ask

The decision this deck asks for: approve AMAFUTA — (1) relaunch Vatika + Amla as a managed oils franchise: unified national price ladder, sachet/LUP formats (90ml and below), UNBS Q-mark + batch-QR authenticity dress; (2) build the moisture-and-scalp story for Afro-textured hair in Luganda-first radio and TikTok — oiling is already the practice, Dabur arrives as its standardised, genuine, affordable version; (3) hold a strict no-go line: no relaxer launch, no extensions, no salon-chemical war with Movit and Darling. Budget shape: trade margin generous, media cheap (the world's lowest data costs), zero listing-fee theatre — the duka stocks what gets asked for.

A US$480M pool in a US$1,300-per-head economy means hair is one of Uganda's largest discretionary categories — hair matters culturally and socially far beyond its wallet share, which is exactly the pattern across Afro-hair markets. The pool splits into three distinct businesses: chemical services (relaxers, salon work — Movit/TCB territory), added hair (Darling's braids and weaves — a manufacturing business), and care and maintenance (oils, moisturisers, shampoos, hair food) — the segment where daily-wallet products live and where Dabur's franchise already has roots.

Brand-by-brand teardown — how the winners actually won (Module 1).

1. Movit (Radiant). Won by making the price point, not just the product: local manufacture beat the tariff wall, "Rooted in Nature, Trusted by Generations" wrapped herbal language in patriotic trust, and a duka-first distribution machine put Radiant relaxers and hair food within a boda ride of everyone. Its teardown lesson is the macro's G5 argument in miniature — in Uganda, the factory is the brand strategy. Weakness: oils are not its heartland; its hair equity is chemical-and-styling era, and the natural-moisture conversation is happening on platforms where Movit's voice is thinner than its shelf.

2. Darling (Godrej/SCD Uganda). Owns added hair by owning the factory (Namanve, since 2002) and the salon relationship — braids are bought by style name, recommended by the braider, and Darling supplies the braider. #FindYourBeautiful gives it aspirational voice. Lesson: in service-mediated segments the stylist is the channel — which is why this deck treats salons as an influence network for oils, not a battleground.

3. TCB / Dark & Lovely (relaxer imports). Hold the salon-chemical tier on brand memory and professional trust. Their flat growth as the natural movement spreads is the cautionary tale for anyone buying into the chemical segment now — the reason the no-go line exists.

4. Samona. The local herbal value floor: NDA/UNBS-certified, priced under everyone, distribution-led. Proves certification + local herbal + low price is a viable formula — and marks the floor Dabur must not chase; there is no margin below Samona.

5. Vaseline/Unilever (hair-adjacent) and the import tier. Unilever's BPC leadership shows what multinational muscle earns here; the import tier (TRESemmé-type SKUs at Kampala supermarkets) shows its limit — full-size international packs price out 90% of the market. The winning international play is therefore international brand at local formats, which is precisely the sachet/LUP architecture.

What the five teardowns say together. Uganda's hair shelf was built by whoever controlled a structural asset: Movit a factory, Darling a factory plus the braider relationship, Samona a price floor, TCB a professional memory, Unilever a distribution machine. Nobody won on advertising alone, and nobody won as a pure importer — the import tier is a Kampala showcase precisely because tariffs price it out of the duka. That is the strategic mirror for Dabur: its structural asset today is accidental (a fragmented import flow plus latent brand equity among Indian-oil-aware shoppers), and AMAFUTA's whole purpose is to convert it into a durable one — first a controlled route to market (W0–W1), then an influence network (the braiders, W2), and eventually, if G5 clears, the same factory logic that built Movit. The second collective lesson is about voice: the local winners speak warmth and belonging, the multinationals speak gloss, and nobody speaks proof. In the most counterfeit-anxious market in this programme, the brand that can demonstrate genuineness — scannably, audibly, repeatedly — takes a seat no incumbent has claimed.

Demand synthesis. The braiding cycle is the category's hidden metronome: every 4–8 weeks, millions of women sit for hours with a braider, scalp exposed and product-decision-ready — take-down, treatment, oiling, re-style. No brand owns that moment. The oils franchise that inserts itself into the braiding cycle — take-down oil, under-braid scalp drops, braider-endorsed — buys repeat purchase on a calendar, which in a daily-coin economy is the closest thing to subscription revenue that exists.

Demand synthesis, part two — the men's file. Ugandan male grooming is early but real: barbershops are as ubiquitous as salons, short-cut maintenance and beard care are creator topics, and Movit already ranges a men's lotion. The oils franchise should hold a men's expression in reserve (beard/scalp oil in the 90ml format, barber-sampled) rather than launch it — the women's braiding economy is where the volume and the repeat rhythm live, and splitting a thin launch budget across genders would starve both. The barber network is mapped in the same W2 fieldwork so the option stays warm.

DEEP THINK — on W1, the formalisation bet. Inversion: guaranteed failure = spending media before controlling supply. Ads that work would pull demand into a shelf still stocked by four uncoordinated importers at four price points — the shopper meets incoherence or a counterfeit, and the campaign funds the grey market. W0's distributor gate is therefore a hard media precondition, not a parallel task. Second-order: when Dabur formalises, the displaced importers don't vanish — they either become sub-wholesalers (the designed outcome, contract them) or they source Vatika from Gulf/Egypt wholesale and undercut (the fight). The counter is designed in: Uganda-specific pack dress (Q-mark + QR + Luganda back panel) makes official stock visibly different, so grey stock self-identifies as suspect in an authenticity-anxious market. That is the quiet genius of leading with genuineness — it weaponises the counterfeit crisis against the grey channel. Base rate: import-brand formalisations in EA succeed when the trade keeps its margin and the brand adds pull (radio-era case history across Kenya/Tanzania FMCG); they fail when brands try to capture the importers' margin in year one. Price the trade generously; take margin later from mix and scale. Kill-switch: macro G3 verbatim — if the beachhead misses at month 12, collapse to Kampala + Jumia and rebuild the price ladder before any further upcountry spend.

DEEP STRAT — the braiding cycle as owned infrastructure. Media plans rent attention; the braiding cycle offers something better — a recurring, physical, product-adjacent moment that arrives every 4–8 weeks for millions of women, mediated by a professional whose recommendation carries near-clinical authority. The W2 design treats braiders the way pharma treats pharmacists: a detailing network with sampling kits, a take-down ritual to codify (oil applied at take-down protects the hairline and preps the scalp — a practice many braiders already half-follow), and margin on direct sales so advocacy pays. At perhaps 500–2,000 active braiders the arithmetic is modest in absolute cost and disproportionate in effect, because each braider touches 15–40 clients a month at the exact moment of product receptivity. Darling proved the channel's power for added hair; nobody has built it for oils. The defensive value matters too: a braider network is slow for Movit to copy without cannibalising its salon-relaxer relationships, and impossible for the grey trade to copy at all — it requires a brand owner who shows up.

Reading T14 as doctrine. The three-column architecture enforces the macro's triptych: the same brand must be buyable with a coin in a village duka, with a note in a town shop, and with a card in Kampala — without the tiers reading as different qualities. The discipline that holds it together is gramage engineering: when costs move, the sachet stays at its coin and shrinks invisibly within tolerances, the 90ml holds its round number as the franchise anchor, and only the Kampala formats carry sticker changes. This is the exact inverse of Gulf price management (where list integrity was the signal and formats were stable) — here the PRICE POINT is the signal and the format flexes. One rule carries over unchanged from Batch 9: no promo depth. At these margins a discount is not a signal, it is a wound.

The channel arithmetic. This table is the deck's honest core: at import economics Uganda hair is a land-grab, not a margin business — contribution single-digit while the franchise, ask-for rate and distribution asset are built. The strategic return sits in the right-hand column (repack flips the duty wall into margin) and in the compounding demographics. A board approving AMAFUTA is approving a two-step investment: buy the franchise now at thin margin, convert the economics at G5. Kuwait taught "never hand the corridor to the trade"; Uganda's lesson is the mirror — there is no corridor, so buy the trade's loyalty with margin and take profit from structure later.

Waterfall read. Two hundred shillings a bottle is the honest launch answer — and the reason the G5 repack study matters more here than any marketing decision: moving the duty line even halfway toward zero triples contribution at identical shelf price. The interim levers are mix (180/200ml formats carry ~8–10%), scale on freight consolidation, and the braiding-cycle repeat rate, which raises volume without raising acquisition cost. What the waterfall forbids is equally clear: no promo depth (there is nothing to give), no listing fees, and no premium-format fantasy — the money is in coins, repeated.

Waterfall sensitivity. Three levers dominate the thin launch economics. Duty structure: the G5 repack scenario is worth more than every marketing decision combined — moving assembly inside the EAC roughly triples unit contribution and unlocks duty-free lanes to Kenya, Tanzania, Rwanda and the Kikuubo re-export flow. Mix: the 180–200ml formats carry roughly double the sachet's contribution rate, so the franchise's health metric is not average price but format pyramid shape — sachets recruit, mid-formats pay. Freight consolidation: eleven scattered shipments a year through four importers is the most expensive possible logistics; a single distributor consolidating quarterly containers takes points off COGS before any negotiation. What does NOT move the needle: media efficiency (already near-free by global standards) and trade-margin trimming (the one lever the deck forbids — the trade IS the strategy at this stage).

A final framing for the board: AMAFUTA is the cheapest BET in the whole 42-country programme by capital at risk, and among the highest by option value. The downside is bounded by the brownfield (worst case, the imports continue as today and the harvest option keeps its small e-commerce profit); the upside compounds through three stacked options — the braiding platform, the EAC manufacture study, and a generation of consumers forming brand habits at median age sixteen. Uganda hair will not pay for itself in the first budget cycle, and this deck refuses to pretend otherwise; what it buys is position in the fastest-compounding consumer market Dabur can enter at this price anywhere on earth.
