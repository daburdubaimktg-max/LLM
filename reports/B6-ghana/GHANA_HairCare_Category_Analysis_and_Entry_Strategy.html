C1 · Category frame — what "hair care" means in Ghana

Ghana is an afro-textured-hair market, and the category's architecture is unlike any of the 35 markets the programme has built: the largest wallet share is not shampoo but the braids/weaves/extensions economy (West Africa is the largest consumer base of a ~US$1.2bn global braiding-extensions market — accio.com 2025, directional, no named primary house); the daily-care layer runs on oils, pomades, leave-ins and "hair foods" — exactly Vatika and Amla's native formats; relaxers persist (Africa-wide relaxer sales still climb even as the US declines — no Ghana-specific decline figure exists, held as a gap); and the whole category floats on a substrate of raw shea butter and alata samina that costs almost nothing and belongs to everyone. The natural-hair movement is real and rising — and contested: local commentary frames natural hair as "hard to manage" for many women, so the deck refuses the flat "Ghana has gone natural" claim and prices both postures.

The natural-hair question, held honestly

The deck refuses the imported storyline that Ghana is mid-"natural revolution." The evidence is double-sided: sourcing names shea, cocoa butter, coconut and baobab as rising locally-anchored ingredients (Statista/6Wresearch framing), while local commentary documents genuine friction — natural hair framed as "hard hair" (nwi a ɛyɛ den), time-poor women defaulting back to protective styles and relaxed maintenance, and Africa-wide relaxer sales still growing even as US litigation collapses that market there. The commercial consequence is precise: the oil lane wins in BOTH postures. A woman transitioning to natural needs oils more, not less; a woman keeping relaxed hair needs scalp and moisture care between retouches; a woman in braids nine months a year needs the under-install scalp lane this deck stakes. Vatika does not need to bet on either side of a contested cultural argument — it sells to the argument's both halves, which is exactly what an entrant with no cultural stake in the fight should do. C3 · Brand-by-brand teardown — how five winners built Ghanaian hair businesses

1 · Darling / X-Pression (Godrej, India) — the origin precedent

Darling began in 1979 in Senegal as a synthetic-braiding-fibre trader; by the time Godrej Consumer Products took 51% (2011) and then 100% of the Ghana business (October 2014), it was the category pioneer across 14 African countries. The teardown lesson is threefold. First, origin: an Indian conglomerate owns and operates Ghana's leading hair-extensions business outright — the single strongest datum that Indian ownership carries no ceiling in Ghanaian hair, exactly as the B6 brief hypothesised (origin India: neutral-to-asset). Second, localisation: Darling won by being everywhere Ghanaian women braid, priced to the market (X-Pression from GH₵25 on Jiji), never marketed as Indian or foreign — origin was infrastructure, not identity. Third, patience: a 45-year-old category built through decades of fibre trading, not a launch blitz. Entry-fee estimate for its moat today: national salon/market coverage plus brand memory measured in generations — unbuyable at any launch budget; the lesson is to borrow the pattern, not attack the position.

2 · Cantu / Shea Moisture / Aunt Jackie's — the diaspora shelf

Three US curl-care houses hold dedicated Jumia GH storefronts (Shea Moisture from GH₵120 on Jiji), selling American formulations of Ghanaian shea back into Ghana at import prices. Their success proves two things the entry needs: Ghanaian urban consumers pay a multiple of raw-shea cost for formulated convenience + efficacy stories, and the e-commerce rail carries premium hair imports viably. Their exposure is the mirror of their strength: a price band (GH₵120+) that excludes the mass market, no traditional-trade presence found, and a story ("shea, but engineered") that a sharp local competitor — or a well-priced Ayurvedic oil house — can undercut on both price and authenticity. They are the ceiling of the import tier; Vatika enters below them, not beside them.

3 · The local shea houses — Nokware, Hamamat, R&R Luxury, Skin Gourmet

Four founder-led Ghanaian brands (2010–2017 vintage) built premium positions on raw authenticity: Nokware ("truth" in Twi) on fair-trade artisan sourcing; Hamamat on the founder's northern shea heritage ("traditionally handmade"); R&R on affordable luxury; Skin Gourmet on radical purity ("no preservatives"). They own the "our shea, done properly" story at boutique scale and diaspora-export prices. The teardown lesson is a boundary: this narrative ground is taken, and it is Ghanaian ground — an Indian entrant who leads with shea claims walks into their home and loses. Vatika's differentiation must be what they cannot say: amla, coconut-milk, cactus — other botanical systems, demonstrated breadth, factory-consistent quality at half the boutique price.

4 · Ghandour Cosmetics — the local-manufacture proof

Founded 1996 by three Lebanese-heritage brothers who chose Ghana deliberately; today the country's #1 local cosmetics manufacturer — ~600 staff, 17 brands, 15 African export markets, GMP + ISO 9001:2015 (brand list unindexed — T18 row). Ghandour proves the B6 brief's local-manufacture flag is real: Ghana can host serious cosmetics production, which is why the macro's lane C (local blending, year-3, evidence-gated) is a live option rather than a hypothetical. It is also the quiet competitive warning: if Vatika's oils succeed, Ghandour can fast-follow with a local look-alike at local cost — the moat must be brand and formulation depth, not shelf presence alone.

5 · Sunsilk (Unilever Ghana) — the incumbent wash-day default

Unilever Ghana (GSE-listed, a century of trading history) carries Sunsilk as the mainstream shampoo default through the deepest distribution in the country (its oral-care network claims ~10,000 micro-retailers — the benchmark for what "national" means). Its weakness is the lane it doesn't hold: Unilever GH shows no oil/treatment franchise of note on the evidence found — wash-day strength, daily-oil absence. That absence is Vatika's opening.

6 · SELF-teardown — Dabur's Ghana hand, honestly

Strengths: the right formats for the market's daily lane (oils, hair foods' logic); the world's largest amla franchise with the "World's No. 1 Hair Oil" claim already leaking into Jiji listings; the Darling origin precedent; the Melcom door; pan-Africa Jumia infrastructure one border away. Weaknesses: zero brand recognition outside the diaspora sliver (no free demand proof — the macro said it plainly); no shea story of its own in a shea-sovereign market; amla is an unknown word in Twi commerce; the 1.47× landed multiple against local blenders; and no sachet evidence either way — the LUP question (first-order for a market whose price memory is trauma-formed) is unresolved until the walk. The plan leans on the strengths without pretending the weaknesses away.

C4 · Consumer & shopper — who buys, who decides, where

The shopper-vs-consumer split runs three ways. The market-woman shopper (Makola/Kejetia stalls) buys for households on bargained prices in a relationship register (the macro's T13 Twi ladder is her language); she decides brands for much of the mass market. The salon/braider professional is the category's hidden gatekeeper: braiders specify the scalp oil, the pre-braid treatment, the take-home; hairdressing is one of the informal economy's largest women's trades (>80% of Ghana's workforce is informal — WIEGO/worldeconomics), and no brand reaches braids without her. The connected urbanite (Accra/Kumasi, Instagram/TikTok-fed, Jumia-comfortable) buys the diaspora shelf and would trade down to an equally credible option at two-thirds the price. Radio in Twi reaches the first; salon activation reaches the second; platform + creator content reaches the third — three engines, one brand story told at three altitudes.

C9 · The substrate stance — amla beside shea, never against it

The deck's positioning law, derived from C3's boundary: Vatika/Amla never claims the shea story. Raw shea is the market's free incumbent (export price aside, the domestic ball price is a T18 walk item), the local houses own its premium telling, and the input's +140% nut-cost spike (2023→H1 2024, oti-gati.com) is quietly raising the "free" floor — time is making formulated oils more competitive without Dabur saying a word. The entry's telling: "Ghana knows shea. India knows amla. Your hair can know both." — a peer-respect register (the programme's twin-heritage grammar in its lightest, ungated cosmetic form; the full gate belongs to OTC where therapeutic claims live). Amla enters as a NEW oil with demonstrated uses, coconut as a familiar one done consistently, cactus as the curiosity SKU that earns TikTok attention. Three real uses per SKU, photographed proof, no borrowed romance.

The braider kit — the gatekeeper program's unit economics

Move 3 deserves its arithmetic shown, because it is the plan's most original spend and its most falsifiable. A kit: one Egg Protein mask 500g, two hero oils, a demo comb, a Twi/English use-card (panel-approved), and a WhatsApp reorder card carrying the distributor's line — authored landed cost ~GH₵95 per kit at the T10 cost structure. Five hundred kits across Accra and Kumasi is a ~GH₵47,500 program — less than a fortnight of radio — and it buys something radio cannot: five hundred professionals whose hands touch thousands of heads a month, each a live demonstration the shopper trusts more than any spot. The falsifier is written into the playbook: if fewer than 30% of kit-holders reorder by month 6, the specifier hypothesis is wrong for Ghana and the budget moves to stall activation — a named retreat, decided by a number, on a date.

The Melcom meeting, staged honestly: the buying desk sees one sheet — six hair SKUs (T2b), four oral, five OTC, four skin — with the founded prices, the FDA registration dates, and the drop-ship terms its own depots already run for "herbal pastes at uniform pricing" (lidercare's phrase for the model Melcom operates today). The ask is not shelf charity; it is a supplier whose paper is clean, whose prices undercut a grey tail the desk can verify on Jiji in thirty seconds, and whose founding family story the room already knows — the meeting's real subject is credit terms and depot allocation, not whether Dabur belongs. That is what the diaspora door is worth: it moves the conversation one stage deeper before it begins.
