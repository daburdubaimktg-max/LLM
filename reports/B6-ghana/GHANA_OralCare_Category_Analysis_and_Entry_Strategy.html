C1 · Category frame — a two-system mouth

Ghanaian oral care runs on two coexisting systems, and the deck's entire strategy flows from respecting both. The first is the toothpaste system: Pepsodent and Close Up (Unilever Ghana) at mainstream, Colgate (imported by Colgate-Palmolive Ghana Ltd from a 51-country supply web led by Thailand 29%, China 22%, India 14% — Volza, directional) with a whitening-and-sensitivity-skewed range, Sensodyne reaching only via cross-border e-tail, Oral-B in brushes. The second is the chewing-stick system: sokodua (Garcinia mannii), with abrofonkateɛ (Terminalia catappa), guava and neem twigs beside it — "most people still patronize this traditional system since it is inexpensive and easily available" (Modern Ghana). This is not a rural residue; it is a mainstream, daily, plural practice whose bark tannins one local source explicitly frames as the tradition's own fluoride. The two systems are not at war — many mouths use both in one week. The entrant that understands this enters beside the stick, never against the paste alone.

One structural note completes the frame: the category's growth mathematics. With a median age of 21.6 and urbanisation at 60%, every year moves cohorts from stick-only childhoods toward paste-including adult routines — the toothpaste system grows demographically without any brand spending a cedi. The entrant's timing question is therefore not "will the paste market grow" but "who will be on the shelf, already trusted, as it does." Founding now — while the herbal lane is one hedge-SKU wide — is the cheap version of that answer; founding after Colgate's hedge becomes a range would be the expensive one.

The whitening signal, and what the fence does with it

The market's whitening appetite is inferred, and the deck says so: the inference source is assortment weight, not a survey — Colgate's Ubuy-visible range runs Optic White, Optic White Purple, Max White One, Charcoal Clean, Enamel Health Whitening, Sensitive Whitening; Unilever relaunched Pepsodent Charcoal in 2024 on an explicit "naturally whiter" promise. Where incumbents stack six whitening SKUs, demand is real (estimate, flagged). The programme's fence answers with its standing position, unchanged across eleven markets: whitening is never sold, claimed or approached — not because Ghana regulates it hard (it may not; the classification gap is open) but because a herbal house that founds its Ghanaian identity on truth-words (nokware) cannot rent the category's most inflation-prone promise. The whitening wallet is left, named and un-fought, to the incumbents who already own its risk. The freshness wallet (anom hua — breath, the register the listening round will hear most, prediction filed) is the entry's adjacent prize instead.

1 · Pepsodent (Unilever Ghana) — the institutional fortress

The teardown lesson: Pepsodent did not buy the market, it built the market's oral-health infrastructure and then owned the credit — the dentist, the school and the state are its media plan. The entry fee to contest this: a decade and the GDA's affection. The correct competitive answer is the one Batch 5 gave Sensodyne's wall twice: refuse the fight. Dabur does not out-institution Unilever in Ghana; it tells a story Unilever's science register cannot — and it does so where Pepsodent's fortress is thinnest: the herbal register and the pharmacy/chemical-seller counter.

5 · The chewing-stick economy — the incumbent that isn't a company

Sokodua's trade is described as "booming in spite of stiff competition" (Modern Ghana) — sticks sell beside toothpaste in every market, are exported as diaspora heritage products on Etsy, and carry a tradition whose grammar (bark, bitterness, morning ritual, no packaging) is the exact inverse of FMCG. The teardown lesson is the deck's central creative law: this tradition is Ghana's, it is alive, and it is not miswak. Garcinia mannii and Salvadora persica are different trees from different civilisations. Dabur's global Miswak paste markets Salvadora "from the resilient landscapes of Africa and South Asia" — a ready-made hook that must be handled with precision here, because a Ghanaian listener who hears "miswak is your chewing stick" will know it is not, and the brand dies of a botany error. The register that works: two traditions, one truth — "Ghana chews sokodua. India chews miswak. Both were right. This paste is that truth, made brushable."

Two further sentences the stick economy earns, because they discipline the whole entry. First: the stick's persistence is not a failure of modernity — dentifrice research keeps finding chewing-stick mechanics genuinely effective for plaque control, and the local framing of bark tannin as tradition's own fluoride is closer to right than wrong; the entry's copy must therefore never imply the stick is inadequate, only that a paste travels where a stick is inconvenient — the office, the journey, the school bag. Second: the stick trade is livelihoods — harvesters, bundlers, market sellers — and an entrant seen to be "replacing" it picks a fight with a supply chain of real families for no commercial gain, since the double-user hypothesis (C11) needs the stick culture healthy, not defeated. The stick is the entry's proof-of-belief, walking around in a million pockets. Leave it exactly where it is.

6 · SELF-teardown — Dabur's oral hand in Ghana

Strengths: the world's largest herbal-toothpaste house entering the one lane no specialist holds; a portfolio spanning price tiers (Herbal, Meswak, Red, Babool); India-origin manufacture already normalised in the category's import stream (Colgate's own 14%); the pharmacy backbone (T5) matching Dabur's strongest programme channel. The chemical-seller counter — the entry's real classroom

The scene the plan is built around: a licensed OTC medicine seller's counter in a district town — one room, painted the licensee's colours, the seller herself the most trusted health voice for a catchment the nearest pharmacist has never visited. Toothpaste sits beside paracetamol and malaria ACTs. When she says "this one is good for bleeding gums," the sentence outsells a month of radio. The macro flagged the OTCMS network's count as a Pharmacy Council written-request item; whatever the number returns (the tens-of-thousands folk figure is NOT cited here — it is unverified), the structural fact stands: Ghana's rural OTC shelf is a human being, not a planogram. The pharmacist listening round therefore runs twice — once in Accra pharmacies, once at district OTCMS counters — and the counter card is written for her voice first, the pharmacist's second.

The buyer's arithmetic, run from their side of the counter

The Georgia oral deck taught the programme to argue a gatekeeper pitch in the buyer's own numbers, and Ghana's version runs like this. A pharma-line distributor carrying Ernest Chemists-class coverage looks at a new herbal paste and sees three numbers: the line's margin (T5's ~16% distributor take is inside pharma norms), the risk of dead stock (answered by the falsifier budget — one quarter's consignment, the supplier's risk, not his), and the question no importer usually answers: who will ask for it? The pitch's answer is the listening round's output — twelve counters' worth of verbatim demand language gathered before the meeting — plus the counter cards that make his retail customers faster at selling it. The distributor is not being asked to believe in Ayurveda; he is being asked to add four rows to an invoice his trucks already deliver, with the demand-creation work visibly pre-done and pre-paid. That is what a four-line supplier with a walk-photographed price card looks like from the buyer's chair, and it is a meeting a one-line hopeful never gets.

One more number belongs to the buyer's side: the January trade calendar. Ghana's retail year peaks in December (Detty December — up to 40% of many retailers' annual revenue) and resets in a thin January where distributor credit is stretched and shelf resets happen. The founding consignment therefore targets a Q1 arrival for an unglamorous reason: the trade has attention and shelf space in the reset window, and a new line negotiated in November lands into January's open planogram rather than fighting December's full one. The calendar is the negotiation's quiet ally.

The two-system demand hypothesis — what the entry is actually betting on

Strip the plan to its load-bearing belief: a mouth that uses both sokodua and Pepsodent in the same week is a mouth that already believes what Dabur Herbal claims — that plants clean, and that convenience matters. The entry does not need to convert stick users to paste or paste users to herbs; it needs the double-users (the overlap of two mainstream systems) to consolidate two beliefs into one tube. That is a smaller ask than any incumbent-share fight, it is unadvertised against (nobody addresses the double-user as a double-user), and it is testable at the counter within one quarter: the listening round's answers will contain it or they won't. If the overlap cohort proves thin — if stick users and paste users are genuinely separate populations — the entry's ceiling drops to the herbal-curious urban segment, the plan's arithmetic already survives that (T11's counter-count math assumed no system-bridging), and the bet is re-scoped honestly. The hypothesis is the upside, not the base.

The queue's discipline note, carried from seven Batch 5 markets and worth restating at the set's midpoint: none of these twelve rows is optional homework. The deck is built so that being wrong on any of them is survivable — but only because the rows are run BEFORE the irreversible commitments, not after. A verification queue that trails a launch is an autopsy; one that precedes it is a method.

DEEP THINK — is the greenfield real, or is the fetch wall painting it?

The deck's found-nothing census under a broken fetch layer is the weakest evidentiary position the programme has planned from, and it is held with both hands. Three scenarios: (a) the greenfield is real — consistent with hair's pattern (where signals DID penetrate the same wall, so oral's silence is meaningful by contrast); (b) a diaspora tail exists below the index's resolution — likely at some scale, changes nothing strategically (founding proceeds; the tail gets badged away as in hair); (c) an organised importer already carries Dabur oral into stalls invisible to the web — the one scenario that would re-shape the plan, and precisely what the G1 walk's first hour answers at Makola's toothpaste stalls. The plan survives all three because its first irreversible commitment (the founding consignment) sits AFTER the walk. Sequencing, again, is the risk management.
