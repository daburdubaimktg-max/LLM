## 1. The analytics consumer
Managers are consumers of analytics; quants are producers. The consumer owns the two ends of the process: framing the problem (hypotheses, relevant variables, shifting ground) and presenting/acting on results. Six-step analytics decision process: (1) recognize the problem/question, (2) review previous findings, (3) model the solution and select variables, (4) collect data, (5) analyze, (6) present and act. Non-quants focus hardest on steps 1 and 6, asking questions all the way through. Build deep, trusting relationships with quants who care about business problems ("effective quantitative decisions are not about the math; they're about the relationships" — Karl Kempf, Intel). Prefer an ROI story when presenting. Cultivate inquiry, not advocacy: never say "find evidence to support my idea"; push analysts to play devil's advocate (Loveman/Caesars). "All models are wrong, but some are useful" (Box). Example of the stakes: a bank lost billions because the mortgage head couldn't understand the analyst's repayment model; TD Bank avoided 2008's worst because its CEO insisted managers understand the math behind products.

## 2. The small-data exercise
A repeatable personal drill that teaches the whole analytics process: (1) Start with something that bothers you at work; write it as a question ("Do meetings really always start late?"). (2) Define the data and collection protocol — definitions matter (when does a meeting "start"?). (3) Collect; refine definitions as gaps appear. (4) Draw a picture — first plot by hand; the go-to is a time-series plot. (5) Develop summary statistics ("10% started on time; average 12 minutes late"). (6) Ask "so what?" — quantify the cost; no so-what, stop. (7) Get a feel for variation — it holds the deeper insight (the VP of finance's meetings all started on time). (8) Pose the next two-three questions and repeat. Use this to become data literate and to coach teams.

## 3. Do you need all that data?
Data factories can churn without improving decisions (the CEO's monthly "book" nobody needed — quarterly exception reporting worked fine). Four questions: (1) Are we asking the right questions — collect what's needed, not what's available? (2) Does our data tell a story — integrate fragments into a coherent business narrative? (3) Does it help us look ahead rather than behind? (4) Do we mix quantitative and qualitative — what's selling AND why?

## 4. Briefing data scientists
Be clear on business impact and the company's ability to act. Watch subtle ambiguity: increase sales ≠ maximize profit (IPA research: ads reducing price sensitivity ≈ 2x more profitable than ads driving volume). Check whether the data already exists (public sources, prior internal analyses). Assess sufficiency and hidden latent factors (loan "vintage effect"). Ask about bias — sample size alone doesn't guarantee validity. Weigh observational studies (cheap, correlation-only) vs experiments (causal, costly, ethical/brand risks — Facebook newsfeed backlash). Count the full cost of data: collection, storage, privacy/reputational risk (de-anonymization is real). Prefer structured, clean data first; unstructured data (~95% of world data) costs extra. KISS: simple techniques first, complexity only when simple fails.

## 5. Designing business experiments
Seven steps: (1) Identify a narrow, testable question that feeds the big question ("does AdWords brand advertising raise monthly sales?" — eBay found its long-standing brand ads had no effect). (2) Use a big hammer — a large intervention so a null result means "customers don't care", not "they didn't notice". (3) Data audit — list internal data on outcomes you want to change AND ones you hope won't change (unintended consequences); add external data. (4) Select a study population representative of the target customers (online users ≠ in-store shoppers). (5) Randomize properly — participants never self-select; treatment and control must differ only by the treatment (Sunday vs Monday coupons fail this). (6) Commit to a plan — sample size, duration, variables — and don't stop early or run until results fit the hypothesis (the replication-crisis mistake). (7) Let the data speak — report all outcomes, including inexplicable ones; ask whether you've found the mechanism; refine and re-run.

## 6. Data vs metrics
DoSomething.org: 1.5M YouTube views, zero donations — they were measuring the wrong thing. A metric is a number tied to your definition of success; data is just numbers. You can't pick your data but you must pick your metrics — and organizations *become* their metrics (players play to what management values: measure views, get views). Vanity metrics (visitors, followers, impressions) are dandelions unless proven to drive the objective. Good metrics: consistent, cheap, quick to collect (rule of thumb: measurable within a week, for free, replicable).
Mauboussin's four steps for picking statistics: (1) define the governing objective (e.g., create economic value); (2) develop a cause-and-effect theory of its drivers (bank: satisfaction → service usage → value) and test the links statistically; (3) identify specific employee-controllable activities that drive those drivers (loan rates, processing speed, teller turnover); (4) re-evaluate regularly — drivers change as customers change (younger base: teller turnover matters less, digital interface more).

## 7. A/B testing
Nearly-100-year-old method (Fisher's agricultural trials → clinical trials → direct mail → web). Mechanics: define the success metric first, randomize users into control/variation, only one systematic difference. Blocking: pre-split by strong known factors (mobile vs desktop) then randomize within. Sequential single-factor tests are suboptimal — they miss interactions (blue wins alone; red+Arial wins together); use multivariate designs (A/B/C/D) with smart subsets. Interpreting: "Control 15% (±2.1), Variation 18% (±2.3)" — the CI means 95% of such ranges would capture the true rate; lift = 3 points; act based on cost of switching. Three classic mistakes: (1) not letting tests run their course (real-time peeking + impatience), (2) tracking hundreds of metrics → spurious correlations (pre-select a few), (3) not retesting — false positives accumulate across many tests; small improvements are the least reliable. Shutterstock lesson: test culture as competitive advantage; their gut said removing watermarks would help — testing proved the opposite.

## 8. Can the data be trusted?
Gold standard: data created under a first-rate quality program with accountabilities, input controls, root-cause elimination — trust it. Otherwise: (a) trace provenance — where was it *created* (not just accessed); check the creating organization's reputation. (b) Friday Afternoon Measurement: spreadsheet 100 records × 10–15 critical elements; red-mark obvious errors; count error-free records. Lots of red = don't use. <5% errors = use with caution. Study error patterns — if 24 of 25 errors sit in one field, drop that field, keep the rest. (c) Clean in three levels: rinse (replace obvious errors/mark missing), scrub (deep, by-hand corrections on a ~1,000-record random sample — be ruthless; this creates your trusted core), wash (automated corrections/imputation on the remainder, done by a competent data scientist). (d) Integration checks: identification (same Courtney Smith across sets), unit/definition alignment (pallets-dollars vs units-euros), de-duplication. Pay special attention when "trusted" and "use with caution" data give different answers — both insight and traps live there. Data needn't be perfect to be useful; know where the flaws are and back off when it isn't good enough.

## 9. Predictive analytics
CLTV, next-best-offer, sales forecasts, ad targeting — all predictive analytics: lots of past data + statistics + assumptions. Data is the usual barrier (single customer view with unique IDs across channels is gold). The workhorse is regression converted into a predictive score. Assumptions are the silent killer: the big one is "the future will be like the past". Models age (Netflix's early-adopter models broke on mainstream users); missing variables bite when they move (2008: models omitted "house prices can fall"). Managers must ask: what are the key assumptions, what would make them invalid, and monitor those factors.

## 10. Regression
Dependent variable = what you want to predict/understand; independent variables = suspected drivers. Plot first (y = outcome, x = driver), fit the line, read the formula (y = 200 + 5x: +1 inch of rain → +5 sales). The error term expresses uncertainty — never ignore it (explaining 10% of variance while acting like it's 90% is how decisions go wrong). Uses: explain (why did service calls drop?), predict (next 6 months' sales), decide (this promo or that?). Correlation ≠ causation: go to the real world to find the mechanism — observe customers, talk to them ("the goal is not to figure out what is going on in the data but what is going on in the world"). Manager mistakes: (1) unfocused fishing expeditions produce fake patterns (flip coins long enough, you'll see streaks); (2) including variables you can't act on distracts (you can't change rain; you can change your promotion); (3) trusting bad data; (4) ignoring the error term; (5) letting data override intuition — if the result makes no sense, question the data and the model.

## 11. Acting on correlations
Key question isn't "is correlation enough?" but "can I act on this correlation?" Two factors: (1) Confidence the correlation recurs = frequency of historical co-occurrence × clarity of causality (fewer plausible explanations → higher confidence). (2) Risk/reward of acting — asymmetric loss functions justify acting on weak correlations when action is cheap and inaction costly (look both ways before crossing). Matrix: high confidence + benefits outweigh costs → act; low confidence + big downside → don't act, or pick the low-risk action. Examples: NYC grease sensors (weak signal, cheap inspection → send the car); PSA screening reversed (weak correlation, no causal clarity, high harm from false positives); supermarket-insurer (red-meat-and-milk buyers are good risks → safe action: targeted marketing; risky action: pricing on buying patterns — brand backlash; investigate causality first to unlock riskier actions). Insight into cause also tells you what changes would break the correlation — monitor those.

## 12. Machine learning fit
Not all automation needs learning: predefined-steps tasks are plain automation. ML = statistical pattern-finding; it knows nothing outside the data you feed it (pneumonia algorithm recommended sending asthmatic patients home — the data hid that they'd been fast-tracked to ICU). Good ML problems: (1) require prediction, not causal inference; (2) are self-contained/insulated from outside shifts. Bad: forecasting a revolutionary product's profits; extrapolating sales when a new competitor just entered. Verify data fit, then interrogate intuition: does the method make conceptual sense for this problem? Set error expectations explicitly (acceptable error rate? forbidden error types?). Harley-Davidson "Albert" case: AI ad platform mined CRM for high-value lookalikes, micro-tested creative, reallocated spend autonomously — leads +2,930% in three months; "call" outperformed "buy" by 447%.

## 13. Statistical significance
Statistical significance quantifies whether a result is likely luck; practical significance asks whether it matters for the business. Sampling error is driven by sample size (more flips, fewer flukes) and population variation (more spread → less confidence at the same n; plot distributions to feel it). Process: state null and alternative hypotheses and a target significance level *before* the test; collect; compute p-value; reject the null if below target. Don't demand lab-grade p-values for business calls: Higgs boson 0.00001, marketing concept maybe 0.25. Confidence intervals: "49% ±3" means 95% of intervals built this way capture the true value — practically, "B leads but not significantly." Large samples can make trivial effects "significant" (0.000001% lift). Redman's warnings: the bigger threat is non-sampling error (broken protocols, lying respondents, lost data, analysis mistakes); use precise language ("statistically significant" vs "strategically significant"); always wed statistics to judgment — "does the result stand up in the market?"

## 14. Nonlinearity
Brains default to straight lines; business curves. The MPG illusion: upgrading 10→20 MPG saves more fuel than 20→50 (500 vs 300 gallons per 10k miles). CPG promo math: paper towels at 50¢/roll, 15¢ marginal cost — 20% off with +20% volume beats 40% off with +80% volume; the deep discount needs +133% volume just to hold profit. Attitude-behavior links are nonlinear (only 5-out-of-5 "concerned" consumers change behavior; averages mask this — a 3/5 mix outperforms uniform 4s). Metric choice changes motivation (days-of-supply vs inventory turns encode the same fact with different perceived returns). Four shapes: (1) gradual-then-steep (CLV vs retention — raising 60%→80% beats 20%→40%, so invest in keepers not just defectors); (2) gradual-then-cliff (mortgage principal); (3) quick-then-plateau (per-unit profit vs volume — overweighting scale, underweighting price); (4) steep-then-gentle (ARR vs payback period). Remedies: build awareness (puzzles, training); focus on outcomes not intermediate indicators (search rank, medium maximization); learn which curve applies in *your* context (satisfaction→retention differs by industry competitiveness); test ≥3 levels in experiments, not 2; map and visualize curves in dashboards and what-if scenarios (the "paceometer" idea).

## 15. Cognitive pitfalls
Heuristics make us feel sound while erring systematically. (1) Confirmation trap — attending to confirming findings (Minnesota Coronary Experiment sat unpublished ~40 years for contradicting fat-heart orthodoxy; employees cherry-pick what bosses want). Escapes: pre-specify data and methods; actively seek disconfirming patterns ("if I'm wrong, what would the data show?"); enlist devil's advocates; don't dismiss sub-threshold findings; independent parallel teams; validate exciting correlations with experiments; treat findings as predictions and test them. (2) Overconfidence — "the mother of all biases"; senior people are most at risk (past success = positive reinforcement); more data increases confidence without increasing accuracy. Escapes: describe the perfect experiment and compare to actual data; formal devil's-advocate step (Kahneman: ask why the analysis might be wrong, every time); pre-mortems (Klein: write next year's failure story); track predictions vs outcomes systematically. (3) Overfitting — models describing noise ("the most important scientific problem you've never heard of" — Silver); Google Flu Trends died correlating flu with "high school basketball". Escapes: train/validation split; pre-specify relationships; keep models simple, effects logical; rule out spurious correlations with common sense; construct alternative narratives from the same data; beware pattern-seeing in randomness (the cold-streak fallacy).

## 16. Outliers
Analytic advocacy = deploying technically accurate numbers to win arguments rather than find truth. Call-center case: "underresourcing" trend was really one policy change affecting 20% of calls (newly retired customers, multi-department approvals) — aggregation concealed the outlier; queuing simulation showed marginal routing changes would fix it. Management, not budget, was the root cause. Harvard-dropout trap: average net worth of dropouts "exceeds" graduates because Gates/Zuckerberg/Land sit in a small denominator. Practice: always ask presenters to identify outliers, how they were defined and handled, and what results look like without them. Hammond's outlier questions: unusual-but-valid value? data-entry error? collected differently or at a different time? Then leave it, or (rarely, after deep investigation) remove/correct — never to make data fit a desired conclusion.

## 17-22. Communication
**17 Davenport:** results never speak for themselves (Mendel's genetics ignored for 35+ years; Gottman scaled marriage research through books/workshops). Roumeliotis's six-part communication frame: business problem → impact measure → available data → initial hypothesis → solution → business impact. Methods detail goes in the appendix.
**18 Stikeleather:** visualize only information that is interpretable (metadata/context intact), relevant to the audience and purpose, and novel. Three legitimate purposes: confirmation (dashboards), education (reporting; intuition-building), exploration (EDA — underused in business). Know your audience, their expectations, and the action the visual should trigger; exploratory visuals should leave questions, educational ones shouldn't. Risks: data quality (garbage in), stripped context, and creator bias (color, positioning, needless 3D).
**19 Duarte's five questions:** (1) Presenting or circulating? Slides get seconds — show conclusions in broad strokes; documents can carry detail for self-paced reading. (2) Right chart for the relationship — bars compare categories; pies only for one-dominant-share points. (3) What's the message — grey the context, one bright color on the point; otherwise everything has equal visual value and nothing matters. (4) Do visuals reflect the numbers — flatten 3D (it misreads 20.4 as ~17), no distorting effects. (5) Memorable — visual metaphors stick (Pollan's glasses of oil for a burger's footprint).
**20 Berinato on uncertainty:** two kinds — statistical uncertainty (value ± confidence; box-and-whisker) and data uncertainty (a range, no accepted visual). Point-estimates look deceptively certain. Probability is hard to feel: 1-in-5 feels likelier than 20% ("image the numerator"); when the 10% event happens the model wasn't wrong — the 10% happened. Election-needle lesson: don't hard-code jitter that reads as live data. Show most-likely AND the range of outcomes to bosses, or they'll read the likelihood as the answer.
**21 Jachimowicz on challenges:** anger often means they care. Steps: take their perspective (their goals may align with yours); collect more data aimed squarely at their criticism; convert challenger into collaborator — buy-in through joint investigation. Two "contradictory" facts can both be true (equal promotion rates AND systematic discrimination in who gets promoted/opportunities); richer data resolves the paradox. Absence of complaints is not absence of a problem — ask what data is *not* being collected.
**22 Morgan on story:** decisions form in the unconscious, where emotion rules and data is absent; the conscious mind rationalizes afterward. Data supports but doesn't persuade (the vendor-switch deck that lost the room vs the quest story that won it). Lead with the story — the alluring future state — then weave in a few key facts. Brands run on stories ("A diamond is forever", "Just do it").
