## 1. The diacritic logic — every mark is a body instruction

Every IAST diacritic is a mechanical instruction for the physical body. Treat them as articulation directives, not decorations.

| Mark | Name | Physical instruction | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| ¯ (overline) | Macron / Dīrgha | Hold the vowel for **2 beats** (mātrās) instead of 1 | ā, ī, ū, ṝ |
| · (under-dot, consonant) | Mūrdhanya / Cerebral / Retroflex | Curl tongue **back and up** to strike the **hard palate** (the dome above the alveolar ridge) | ṭ, ṭh, ḍ, ḍh, ṇ, ṣ |
| · (under-dot on r/l) | Vocalic | Vowel produced by tongue **vibrating near** the hard palate (not striking) | ṛ, ṝ, ḷ |
| · (under-dot on h) | Visarga | A **soft echo** of the preceding vowel — voiceless | ḥ |
| ´ (acute) | Tālavya / Palatal | Tongue blade contacts the **soft palate** (middle roof) | ś |
| ˜ (tilde) | Palatal nasal | Tongue blade against soft palate, nasalized | ñ |
| ˙ (over-dot, n) | Guttural nasal | Back of tongue against velum, nasalized | ṅ |
| ˙ (over-dot, m) | Anusvāra | Pure nasal vibration, no closure | ṃ |
| ʼ (apostrophe) | Avagraha | Marks an **elided 'a'** — pause or extend previous vowel | so 'ham → so'ham |
| 3 (numeral) | Pluta | Hold vowel for **3 beats** | O3m |

### The cardinal rule

**An under-dot in the wrong place changes the meaning of the śāstra.** *vana* (forest) is not *vaṇa*. *kṛṣṇa* is not *krishna*. Be precise.

## 2. Vowel mechanics — the independent life-force (svarāḥ)

Vowels are independent. They do not need the support of any consonant to be pronounced. In Devanāgarī they have a full independent form (अ आ इ ई उ ऊ ऋ ॠ ऌ ए ऐ ओ औ).

### Short vowels (Hrasva — 1 beat / mātrā)

| IAST | Sound | Mouth position |
|---|---|---|
| **a** | "u" in *but* (a schwa) | Throat, mouth slightly open |
| **i** | "i" in *bit* | Soft palate, tongue mid-front |
| **u** | "u" in *put* | Lips rounded, tongue back |
| **ṛ** | "ri" in *rim* with guttural edge | Tongue vibrates near hard palate (NOT a consonant) |
| **ḷ** | "lri" — vocalic l, very rare | Tongue vibrates near teeth ridge |

### Long vowels (Dīrgha — 2 beats)

| IAST | Sound | Mouth position |
|---|---|---|
| **ā** | "a" in *father* | Throat, mouth open, sustained 2 beats |
| **ī** | "ee" in *seen* | Soft palate, tongue front, 2 beats |
| **ū** | "oo" in *root* | Lips rounded, tongue back, 2 beats |
| **ṝ** | Long vocalic ṛ (very rare) | Tongue vibrates near hard palate, 2 beats |

### Always-long vowels (diphthongs — 2 beats)

These four are **never short**. They begin in one place and end in another within the 2-beat hold.

| IAST | Sound | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| **e** | "ay" in *say* | Begins guttural, ends palatal |
| **ai** | "ai" in *aisle* | Begins guttural, ends further palatal |
| **o** | "o" in *go* | Begins guttural, ends labial |
| **au** | "au" in *cow* | Begins guttural, ends further labial |

### Pluta — 3 beats

When a numeral **3** appears next to a vowel (e.g., **O3m**, *oṃ* extended), hold for **three beats** — used in invocation, calling, dramatic emphasis.

### Verification: the four vowel categories

When you encounter any vowel in IAST, classify it:
- 1 beat: **a, i, u, ṛ, ḷ**
- 2 beats: **ā, ī, ū, ṝ, e, ai, o, au**
- 3 beats: any vowel followed by **3**

## 3. Consonant mechanics — the fractured elements (vyañjanāni)

All consonants are **half-sounds** (*halanta*). They are fractured — they cannot be pronounced without a vowel to carry them. *k* alone is unpronounceable. *k + a = ka* makes the sound complete.

### The 25 stop consonants — organized as a 5×5 grid

The classical grid is the heart of Sanskrit phonetics. Each row is a *varga* (class) defined by **sthāna** (point of origin). Each column is defined by **prayatna** (effort: voicing + aspiration).

|                | Unvoiced unaspirated | Unvoiced aspirated | Voiced unaspirated | Voiced aspirated | Nasal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Guttural** (*kaṇṭhya*, throat)           | **k** | **kh** | **g** | **gh** | **ṅ** |
| **Palatal** (*tālavya*, soft palate)        | **c** | **ch** | **j** | **jh** | **ñ** |
| **Cerebral** (*mūrdhanya*, hard palate)     | **ṭ** | **ṭh** | **ḍ** | **ḍh** | **ṇ** |
| **Dental** (*dantya*, teeth)                | **t** | **th** | **d** | **dh** | **n** |
| **Labial** (*oṣṭhya*, lips)                 | **p** | **ph** | **b** | **bh** | **m** |

### The semivowels (*antaḥsthā* — "in-between")

Each is associated with a *sthāna*:
- **y** — palatal (soft palate)
- **r** — cerebral (hard palate)
- **l** — dental (teeth ridge)
- **v** — labio-dental (lips and teeth)

### The sibilants (*ūṣman* — "warm/breathy")

Three sibilants, one for each of the upper articulation points:
- **ś** — palatal "sh" (soft, breathy — *Śiva, śānti*)
- **ṣ** — cerebral/retroflex "sh" (tongue curled back — *Kṛṣṇa, Viṣṇu, Pūṣṇa*)
- **s** — dental "s" (clean, English "s" — *sāra, satya*)

### The aspirate

- **h** — pure breath, voiced. Originates in the throat.

### Total

5 vargas × 5 = 25 + 4 semivowels + 3 sibilants + 1 aspirate = **33 consonants**.

## 4. The five points of origin (sthāna)

This is the most important concept in śikṣā. Every consonant must be produced at its assigned point. Mixing points (e.g., pronouncing *ṭ* as English *t*) is the most common Western error and changes meaning.

### Guttural (Kaṇṭhya — throat)

- **Letters:** k, kh, g, gh, ṅ, h, a, ā, viṣarga (ḥ — partial)
- **Mechanism:** Back of tongue contacts the velum (soft palate at the very back).
- **English analog:** "k" in *kick*, "g" in *go*, but with cleaner contact.
- **Note:** The vowel *a* is also guttural — it originates in the throat, mouth open.

### Palatal (Tālavya — soft palate)

- **Letters:** c, ch, j, jh, ñ, y, ś, i, ī, e, ai (partial)
- **Mechanism:** Blade of tongue (just behind the tip) contacts the **soft middle palate**.
- **English analog:** "ch" in *church*, "j" in *jam* — but cleaner. *c* is *not* "k"; it's "ch".
- **Crucial:** *ś* is the **soft "sh"** here — the *sh* of *Śiva*, gentle.

### Cerebral / Retroflex (Mūrdhanya — hard palate)

- **Letters:** ṭ, ṭh, ḍ, ḍh, ṇ, r, ṣ, ṛ, ṝ
- **Mechanism:** **Tip of tongue curls back and up**, striking the **dome of the hard palate** (further back than English alveolar t/d).
- **English analog:** None natively. American English "r" (*rod*) approaches the position. Indian English speakers do this naturally.
- **Crucial distinction:** The under-dot under t/ṭ, d/ḍ, n/ṇ, s/ṣ is **the** retroflex marker. *Kṛṣṇa* has retroflex *ṣ* AND retroflex *ṇ* — both with curled tongue.

### Dental (Dantya — teeth)

- **Letters:** t, th, d, dh, n, l, s, ḷ
- **Mechanism:** **Tip of tongue touches the back of the upper front teeth**.
- **English analog:** Spanish or French "t/d" — softer, more forward than English. English speakers usually default to alveolar position; pull forward to the teeth.
- **Crucial:** *th* here is **t + breath**, never the *th* in *the* or *think*. Think of *boat-house* compressed.

### Labial (Oṣṭhya — lips)

- **Letters:** p, ph, b, bh, m, u, ū, o, au (partial), v (partial — labio-dental)
- **Mechanism:** Lips meet (or, for v, lower lip touches upper teeth).
- **English analog:** Direct.

### Quick-tap audit

When you encounter a consonant, ask: which of the five points produces it? Answer must be one of {throat, soft palate, hard palate, teeth, lips}. If you can't answer, you don't yet know how to pronounce it.

## 5. Aspiration — the breath of air

The aspirated consonants — **kh, gh, ch, jh, ṭh, ḍh, th, dh, ph, bh** — are **not** two letters. They are a **single consonant followed by a distinct puff of air** before the next vowel.

### How to test

Hold a hand 2 inches in front of the mouth. Say *pa* — barely any air. Say *pha* — strong puff hits the hand. That's the aspiration.

### Common Western errors

- **th** mistaken for English *th* in *the*. **NO.** Sanskrit *th* = *t* + breath. Like *boat-house* run together fast.
- **ph** mistaken for English *f*. **NO.** Sanskrit *ph* = *p* + breath. Like *up-hill* run together.
- **kh** treated as silent *k* + *h*. **NO.** A clean *k* with a puff of air, like *ink-horn*.
- **ch** mistaken for English *ch* in *church*. **NO.** That's plain *c*. *ch* = *c* + breath.

### The 10 aspirates and their unaspirated partners

| Unaspirated | Aspirated |
|---|---|
| k (*kuṭa*) | kh (*kha*, sky) |
| g (*gaja*, elephant) | gh (*ghana*, cloud) |
| c (*caraṇa*, foot) | ch (*chala*, deceit) |
| j (*jana*, person) | jh (rare) |
| ṭ (*ṭīkā*, commentary) | ṭh (*ṭhakkura*, master) |
| ḍ (*ḍamaru*, drum) | ḍh (*ḍhakka*, drum) |
| t (*tat*, that) | th (*ratha*, chariot) |
| d (*dāna*, charity) | dh (*dharma*, duty) |
| p (*pāda*, foot) | ph (*phala*, fruit) |
| b (*bala*, strength) | bh (*bhakti*, devotion) |

## 6. Special markers

### Visarga (ḥ)

- IAST: **ḥ** (h with under-dot)
- Devanāgarī: **ः**
- Sound: a **soft, voiceless echo of the preceding vowel**, NOT a hard "h".

**Reading rule:** *namaḥ* is read *na-ma-ha* — but the final *ha* is faint, like an exhale, almost a sigh. It is NOT *namaha* with three full syllables of equal weight. Some traditions render it as *na-ma-h* with the vowel-color of the preceding vowel.

**Examples:**
- *namaḥ* = *na-ma-(h)a* (echo of 'a')
- *hariḥ* = *ha-ri-(h)i* (echo of 'i')
- *guruḥ* = *gu-ru-(h)u* (echo of 'u')

The visarga "borrows" the color of the preceding vowel for its echo.

### Anusvāra (ṃ)

- IAST: **ṃ** (m with under-dot — or sometimes ṁ with over-dot)
- Devanāgarī: **ं** (small dot above)
- Sound: a **pure nasal vibration** — the sound your nose makes when you hum *mmm* with mouth slightly open.

**Reading rule:** Anusvāra adapts to the consonant that follows.
- Before a labial (*p, ph, b, bh, m*) → close the lips, like *m*. *saṃsāra* → *sam-sā-ra*? No — *saṃ* with nasal *m*-color.
- Before any other consonant → keep vibration in nasal cavity, mouth open.

**Common examples:**
- *Oṃ* / *Auṃ* — held nasal at the end
- *saṃsāra* — *saṃ-sā-ra* (nasal vibration on first syllable)
- *aham* (with nasal final) — final m may be written *ahaṃ* in some texts when a vowel follows.

### Avagraha (ʼ — sometimes typed as a curly apostrophe ')

- IAST: **ʼ** or **'** (apostrophe)
- Devanāgarī: **ऽ**
- Function: Marks an **elided 'a'** that has been dropped due to *sandhi* (sound-meeting rules).

**Reading rule:** Pronounce a brief pause OR extend the preceding vowel by one beat. Do NOT just concatenate the words.

**Examples:**
- *so 'ham* (originally *saḥ aham*) → *so* (held) *'ham*. The apostrophe marks where the *a* of *aham* was elided.
- *te 'pi* (originally *te api*) → *te* (held) *'pi*.
- *ko 'pi* (originally *kaḥ api*) → *ko* (held) *'pi*.

**Common Western error:** Reading *so'ham* as one word *soham*. Wrong. The avagraha is a phonetic instruction.

### Pluta (3)

- IAST notation: vowel followed by **3** — e.g., **O3m**, **rāma3**, **bho3ḥ**
- Function: Hold the vowel for **three mātrās** (beats) instead of one or two.
- Use: Calling out, invocation, dramatic emphasis. Most commonly seen in *Auṃ* / *O3m* in invocation contexts.

## 7. Sandhi essentials — what happens when sounds meet

*Sandhi* (junction) is the system of phonetic changes that occurs when sounds meet at word boundaries. A full treatment is beyond this skill's scope, but these are the most-encountered patterns relevant to reading Bhaktivedanta IAST:

### Vowel sandhi at boundaries

- *saḥ + aham* → *so 'ham* (visarga + a → o, with avagraha marking the dropped a)
- *te + api* → *te 'pi*
- *na + iti* → *neti* (a + i → e)
- *na + uta* → *nota* (a + u → o)
- *mahā + ātmā* → *mahātmā* (ā + ā → ā, no change in sound, just merge)

### Visarga sandhi (very common)

- *aḥ + voiced consonant* → *o* (e.g., *rāmaḥ + gacchati* → *rāmo gacchati*)
- *aḥ + unvoiced consonant* → *aḥ* (preserved)
- *aḥ + a* → *o 'a* — written as *o 'a* (the avagraha appears)

These are the rules that explain why you frequently see *o* and apostrophes in the middle of Bhaktivedanta verse text. They are NOT typos.

## 9. Quick-reference articulation card

When asked to render a phonetic breakdown quickly, use this mental decision tree:

```
For each syllable:
  1. What's the vowel? → 1, 2, or 3 beats?
  2. What consonant(s) precede it? → Which sthāna (throat/soft-palate/hard-palate/teeth/lips)?
  3. Aspirated? → Add a puff-of-air note.
  4. Retroflex? → Note the curled tongue.
  5. Sibilant? → Distinguish ś (soft) vs ṣ (retroflex) vs s (dental).
  6. End-marker? → ḥ = soft echo. ṃ = nasal vibration. ʼ = elided a.
```

This is the entire skill in one card. Master it, and Sanskrit articulation becomes mechanical and reliable.
