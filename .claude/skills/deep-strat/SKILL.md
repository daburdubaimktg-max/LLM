# DEEP STRAT

A full strategic analysis framework for non-trivial business decisions — category moves, channel shifts, brand plays, organizational bets, portfolio choices, competitive responses.

## When to trigger

Fire this skill whenever the user includes the phrase **DEEP STRAT** (any casing) anywhere in their message. The phrase is binding — do not skip the framework or deliver a lighter version even if the surrounding question seems simple, familiar, or easily answered. When the user reaches for DEEP STRAT, they are explicitly asking for the full senior-consultant treatment, not a quick take.

If critical inputs are missing (the decision itself, the candidate options, or the commercial context), ask **one** clarifying question before running the framework. Then proceed.

## Operating principles

- **Voice:** senior CPG strategist speaking to a Marketing Head. Warm, direct, confident. No corporate hedging, no "it depends" without a follow-on.
- **Format:** clear section headers, structured bullets within each section, minimal long prose.
- **So-what discipline:** every observation must connect to an implication for the decision. Raw facts without "so what" don't earn space.
- **Quantify wherever possible:** even rough ranges and orders of magnitude. Unquantified strategic analysis is where bad CPG decisions hide.
- **Named specificity over abstraction:** "Vatika's SoV in KSA shampoo runs ~15 pts below Pantene" beats "the category is competitive."
- **Rejection is part of the output:** what we refuse to do is as important as what we choose.

## What this skill produces

A six-part analysis in this exact order:

1. **Decision Scoping** — pre-flight
2. **Analysis A** — Three-Persona Stress Test
3. **Analysis B** — Assumption Audit (five domains)
4. **Analysis C** — Blind-Spot Scan (five rounds)
5. **Master Ranking Table** — RAG-coded with decision metadata
6. **Commitment Block** — WILL DO / WON'T DO / Reputation Bet

## 1. Decision Scoping (pre-flight)

Before any analysis, pin down the following five lines. One sentence each.

- **The decision** — What is actually being decided? Not the topic — the specific choice between specific options.
- **Time horizon** — When does this pay off? (0–3 months / 3–12 months / 12m+)
- **Reversibility** — Two-way door (reversible in ≤90 days with limited cost) or one-way door (hard to undo)?
- **Owner** — Who carries the outcome? Nishant, a brand lead, a cross-functional group, the agency?
- **Success test** — What single metric or observable outcome at 90 days tells us this worked?

If any line is unclear, flag the ambiguity explicitly. A fuzzy decision produces fuzzy analysis, and the rest of the framework compounds the fuzz.

---

## 2. Analysis A — Three-Persona Stress Test

Run each option through three independent perspectives before synthesizing. Do not let the personas contaminate each other — give each a clean hearing.

### The Skeptic
The senior colleague who has seen this movie before and expects it to fail. Name their **three strongest objections**. Specificity is the quality bar: "market is crowded" is weak; "Pantene and Dove together hold ~55% SoV in KSA shampoo and this spend doesn't close that gap in 12 months" is strong.

### The Champion
The executive or brand lead most likely to own delivery. Name their **three strongest reasons this is the right move**. What are they seeing that the Skeptic is missing — category whitespace, consumer tension, organizational timing, a shift in distribution?

### The End User
The consumer, shopper, or customer this ultimately serves. Strip out boardroom logic. Does this actually make their life, their shelf moment, their purchase decision, or their product experience materially better? If the End User would shrug, flag it — regardless of how strong Skeptic and Champion are.

### Convergence Ranking
Where do all three personas independently agree? Those convergence points are the closest thing to truth in the analysis. Rank them in order of conviction. Disagreement isn't failure — it localizes the real debate.

### Synthesis
One paragraph, maximum five sentences: what the three-persona view collectively reveals about this decision, and where the live debate now sits.

---

## 3. Analysis B — Assumption Audit

List the critical assumptions behind each option across **five domains**. For each, tag danger level **if the assumption turns out to be wrong**:

- 🟢 **LOW** — worth noting; limited damage if wrong
- 🟡 **MED** — meaningful; revisit during execution
- 🔴 **HIGH** — if this is wrong, the plan fails

For every **HIGH** assumption, name a **cheap test** that could de-risk it before major commitment (a targeted consumer read, a pricing experiment in one market, a trade conversation, a small digital pilot).

### Customer
What we're assuming about who they are, what they want, what they'll pay, and how they actually behave versus claim to behave.

### Market / Category
What we're assuming about category dynamics, pricing corridors, regulatory environment, channel mix shifts, and demand elasticity.

### Team / Organization
What we're assuming about internal capability, leadership appetite, cross-functional alignment, capacity, and agency bandwidth.

### Competitor
What we're assuming about what competitors will — and critically, **won't** — do in response. Include both the obvious responders and the quiet ones.

### P&L / Capital
What we're assuming about cost envelope, return, payback period, margin impact, and the opportunity cost of not doing the best alternative use of the same money.

**Output format for each assumption:**

`[Domain] — [Assumption stated in one line] — Danger: LOW / MED / HIGH — [If HIGH: cheap test to de-risk]`

## 4. Analysis C — Blind-Spot Scan

Five rounds. Apply each to every option on the table.

### Round 1 — Cool vs Needed
Is this option **actually required** to hit the objective, or is it interesting, novel, fashionable, or award-friendly? Distinguish "this would be cool" from "this moves the number." Be ruthless. CPG marketing burns unusual amounts of money on the former dressed up as the latter.

### Round 2 — Wrong User
Who are we accidentally designing for? The agency creative lead? Internal stakeholders who want to feel modern? A persona who isn't actually the primary buyer or decision-maker in our category? Name the real user we should be designing for and whether the current option actually serves them.

### Round 3 — Payoff Test
Force a rough calculation: **(Size of prize × Probability of success) ÷ (Effort + cost)**. Does the math actually justify the work? If the prize is small, the probability is low, or the effort is disproportionate, say so plainly. Prefer explicit numbers or ranges over qualitative vibes.

### Round 4 — Inversion
**What would need to be true for this to fail catastrophically?** Reverse the question — this surfaces the failure modes pattern-matching misses. If three of your inversion answers are plausible, the plan is fragile, even if the headline case looks strong.

### Round 5 — Second-Order Effects
Every move creates reactions. Map them:
- **Competitors:** what do the two or three most likely responders do in the 0–6 month window?
- **Channel:** what do retailers, distributors, e-commerce and quick-commerce platforms do — on listings, on promo support, on terms?
- **Internal:** what does our own team, sales force, and agency do — what gets dropped, what gets oversubscribed, what politics surface?

## 5. Master Ranking Table

Bring every option into a single table. Rank from most to least recommended. Use RAG coding:

- 🟢 **GREEN** — top recommendation, proceed with conviction
- 🟡 **YELLOW** — conditional, proceed only after a specific de-risking step lands
- 🔴 **RED** — reject or deprioritize

Every row must include these columns:

| Option | RAG | Time Horizon | Reversibility | P&L Magnitude (rough) | First-7-Days Action |

Column definitions:

- **Time Horizon** — 0–3m / 3–12m / 12m+
- **Reversibility** — two-way door / one-way door
- **P&L Magnitude** — rough range (e.g., "+$500K–1.5M revenue in Yr1," "−$200K opex," "breakeven at Month 9"). Only use "unknown" when genuinely so, and flag it as an assumption to close.
- **First-7-Days Action** — the single most important move in Week 1 if this option is chosen. This is the bridge from strategy to motion; without it, GREEN options rot on slide decks.

Order rows by preference (GREEN at top, RED at bottom). If multiple options share a colour, rank within the colour by conviction.

---

## 6. Commitment Block

Close every DEEP STRAT output with three lines. Keep it tight — this is where strategy becomes decision.

### WILL DO
The GREEN option, stated as a **verb-led commitment** with a named owner and a date. "Launch Eid value-toned creative in KSA with MCN by [date], led by [owner]." Not "explore the possibility of…" or "consider shifting towards…" Hedged language belongs in the analysis, not the commitment.

### WON'T DO
The RED paths explicitly rejected, each with a one-line reason. Strategy is as much the refusal as the choice — stating the refusal makes the commitment real and prevents scope creep from quietly reviving what was rejected.

### Reputation Bet
One sentence, first-person, stating the single call the user would stake their credibility on. Format: **"If I'm wrong about [specific claim], I'll [specific consequence]."** This forces conviction and prevents intellectual fence-sitting. If the Reputation Bet feels uncomfortable to write, the analysis isn't done yet.

---

## Scope note

Use the full framework for genuine strategic questions — category moves, brand repositioning, channel shifts, portfolio bets, organizational plays, major competitive responses, M&A, regional strategy. Don't run the full seven-course meal on small tactical questions (agency briefs, single-SKU pricing tweaks, minor creative decisions). For those, a lighter hand is correct. The **DEEP STRAT** trigger is the user's explicit signal that they want the full treatment — respect the trigger when it appears.
