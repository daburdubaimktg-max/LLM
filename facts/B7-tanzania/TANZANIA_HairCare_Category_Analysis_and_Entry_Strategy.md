# FACTS — TANZANIA_HairCare_Category_Analysis_and_Entry_Strategy
_source file: reports/B7-tanzania/TANZANIA_HairCare_Category_Analysis_and_Entry_Strategy.html_

**THE PLAY:** FOCUS. One line: build Dabur's Tanzania hair business on the one asset no competitor has industrialised — coconut oil, which Tanzanians already call by name ( mafuta ya nazi ), already trust from Zanzibar tradition, and can currently buy only as artisanal bottles or anonymous bulk. This deck derives the third distinct East-African hair strategy in three countries: Uganda was a Movit-flank, Kenya was an Amla-franchise defence; Tanzania is a category-creation bet — take a habit that pre-exists the brand and give it its first mass-market FMCG form.

**THE PLAY:** DEEP THINK — inverting the bet. Invert: what would have to be true for the coconut white space to be a mirage rather than an opportunity? Three candidate explanations for the empty shelf. (1) The belief is satisfied informally — bulk oil at the market measures cheaper per ml than any branded bottle can, so the mass consumer sees no reason to pay for a label. This is the serious one, and it is why the sachet is the spearhead: the sachet does not ask her to abandon bulk economics, it asks for TZS 300–500 once, against a guarantee of purity that bulk cannot make (and adulteration of loose oil is a live consumer anxiety the counterfeit data makes rational). (2) Marico and others assessed and passed — plausible, but the observable reasons (thin wallet, weak modern trade, no CEPA) are precisely the frictions Dabur's duka-first, India-corridor model is built for; the pass of an incumbent optimised for modern trade is not evidence against a traditional-trade entrant. (3) CCIL knows something we don't — the champion sits on the plant, the fleet and the local read, and has NOT industrialised nazi. Possibly a relaxer-era blind spot; possibly a queued launch. The 90-day CCIL intelligence file (T21) exists to close exactly this question, and the kill-switch (T19) prices the possibility that explanation (1) wins. Second-order: if Dabur's launch works, the most likely response is not a Movit price war but a CCIL fast-follow with local-manufacture economics — which is why the plan front-loads the assets a fast-follower cannot copy in a year: TANOGA recordation, the radio register, and the Zanzibar gesture.

**THE PLAY:** DEEP STRAT — the braid-aftercare wedge, engineered. The single most valuable structural fact in this category is that its biggest spender (the braid wearer) buys her style and her care in different transactions from different sellers. Darling monetises the install; nobody systematically monetises the 6–8 weeks under the braid — itch, dryness, edge stress — during which oil is the canonical answer. The wedge design: (i) a 500-stylist programme across Dar/Mwanza/Arusha, recruited through braid-supply wholesalers, trained on a 10-minute under-braid oiling ritual with Vatika sachets as the take-home; (ii) the stylist earns a margin on sachet strips sold chair-side — making her a distributor, not a billboard; (iii) the radio platform carries the same ritual nationally so the salon lesson and the duka purchase reinforce each other; (iv) explicitly no braid hardware, ever — the programme must read to Darling as complementary traffic, not attack, because Darling's salon network is worth more as an open channel than as a provoked competitor. Works if: stylists adopt the ritual because it earns them money and repeat visits. Fails if: sachet chair-side economics are too thin to hold stylist attention — in which case the programme degrades gracefully into sampling, which at Tanzanian sachet costs is still the cheapest qualified-trial machine available. Kenya ran 800 stylists on an established brand; Tanzania runs 500 on a created category — smaller, earlier, and doing a harder job: teaching the branded form of a known behaviour.
