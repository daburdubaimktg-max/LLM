One line: Rwanda's health system is the batch's most complete — 91% insured, 30,000 community health workers treating 60% of malaria, a declining private pharma market — which means the episodic OTC demand every other market's deck organises is largely NATIONALISED here; this deck therefore SELECTS: three narrow lanes the state does not serve (daily wellness supplements through the new Apr-2025 registration guideline, the Honitus herbal-cough flank, and a Chyawanprash diaspora beachhead through Kigali's Indian grocers), one deliberate refusal (honey — the fifth distinct verdict in five markets), and a channel strategy built on the two operators the moment favours: Goodlife's expanding pharmacy chain and Kasha's women-first platform.

**THE PLAY:** DEEP THINK — the declining market, inverted. A shrinking category topline usually kills an entry deck; this one survives inversion because the decline's CAUSE is the opportunity's proof. Decompose the −3.86%: it is donor-and-state substitution — Mutuelle deepening, community treatment expanding, subsidised supply chains crowding out private episodic purchases. Now ask what that same machinery produces as a by-product: a population with the region's highest health-system trust, rising NCD awareness (the system diagnoses what it then can't fully treat), lengthening life expectancy, and a salaried Kigali tier whose health needs are migrating from episodes (state-served) to maintenance (nobody-served). The declining line measures the state eating the OLD market while the NEW one — wellness, prevention, regimen — forms outside the statistic. Second-order check: could the state nationalise wellness too? In principle (supplement subsidies exist elsewhere); in practice the fiscal logic runs the other way — a state absorbing episodic costs WANTS private prevention spending, and Rwanda's own NCD strategy language invites it. The kill test: if Goodlife's build-out stalls (the private wellness shelf failing to materialise), the apex thesis loses its channel evidence and the deck's kill-switch shrinks the plan before capital follows the error. Watching one chain's store count as a thesis-health metric is unusually clean — and it is exactly what the quarterly review does.

**THE PLAY:** DEEP STRAT — the Goodlife partnership, engineered before the leverage flips. The design problem: a chain at 16 stores heading to 35 needs brands now and will dictate terms later. The structure: (i) the wellness corner — a co-designed 1-metre herbal-wellness set (Dabur-anchored, not Dabur-exclusive) that gives Goodlife a differentiated category story its supermarket rivals cannot copy, in exchange for category-captain input on adjacency and pricing; (ii) the growth clause — terms priced at today's 16 stores with automatic extension to new stores at the same economics, so the chain's growth compounds Dabur's distribution without renegotiation; (iii) the training layer — pharmacist wellness modules (regimen counselling, claim boundaries) that make Goodlife's staff the category's educators — the governed market's analogue of Tanzania's dispenser programme, executed through a private chain because Rwanda's equivalent public channel (CHWs) is correctly off-limits to brands; (iv) the data covenant — monthly category sell-through, the apex thesis's only real-time instrument. Works if: Goodlife values category authorship over slotting-fee extraction at this stage of its build-out (chains in expansion usually do). Fails if: the chain demands exclusivity — refused per the risk board, with Kipharma + Kasha as the plurality guarantee. The clock matters more than the terms: every quarter of delay adds stores to the chain's leverage and subtracts from Dabur's founding-partner claim.
